Front matter

Preface

A building is a promise. The people who walk into a classroom, a clinic waiting room or a plant floor trust that the roof will hold, the exits will open, the alarm will sound and the air is fit to breathe. Someone keeps that promise every day, usually without being noticed, and usually with less money than the building needs.

That someone is the facility manager, supported by inspectors, engineers, code officials, custodians, technicians and contractors. Their work sits where engineering, law, finance and plain human attention meet. Most of them were never taught the whole picture. They learned a trade, then inherited a portfolio.

This handbook tries to give them the whole picture. It teaches how buildings work, how they fail, how to look at them without hurting yourself or overstepping your qualifications, how to write down what you find so that someone can act on it, and how to work out which laws, codes and standards actually apply. It is written for beginners but does not shy from the detail that experienced professionals need.

Two commitments shape every page. First, accuracy over coverage. Where a requirement could not be verified, the book says so and sends you to the authority that decides. Second, honest boundaries. A visual walk-through is not a substitute for specialized testing or professional engineering, and a textbook is not a license.

Who this is for

Facility teams

Facility managers, maintenance supervisors, operations directors and technicians in school districts, universities, hospitals, offices, housing and industry.

Owners and boards

School boards, property owners, association boards and executives who approve budgets and need to read a condition report with confidence.

Inspectors and code staff

Building, fire and housing inspectors, and consultants who want a consistent method and a shared vocabulary with the people they inspect.

Contractors and engineers

Design and construction professionals who hand over buildings and want to know how owners will operate and maintain them.

Students and trainees

College and continuing-education learners in facilities, construction and building-science programs. Every chapter ends with exercises and a quiz.

Public agencies

Government facility groups, housing authorities and safety officers who work within procurement, funding and public-accountability rules.

Scope and limitations

This edition covers the foundations of facility management, the major building systems, the professional inspection process, and life safety, accessibility and environmental health. School buildings are the main worked setting, and the principles carry to other occupancies. It explains common principles first, then what changes by occupancy, building type, jurisdiction and risk level. Commercial, residential and industrial facilities, budgeting and capital projects, and facility technology are outside the scope of this edition.

Read before relying on any requirement
  • This book supports professional learning. It does not establish legal compliance, confer a license, replace a required inspection or override the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ).
  • Model codes and consensus standards are law only where a government has adopted them. Edition and local amendments decide the actual requirement.
  • Louisiana and New Orleans are the worked example. Nothing about New Orleans is assumed to apply across Louisiana, and no edition is assumed to be in force without a source check. Dates of checks are shown.
  • No chapter contains real inspection results, code approvals or agency endorsements. Photographs are not used. Drawings are conceptual and labeled. Case studies and data are fictional.
  • The book has not won an award and has no endorsement from any government agency or standards body.
Safety boundary

Nothing here instructs an unqualified reader to enter a confined space, open energized equipment, disturb suspected asbestos, collect hazardous samples, bypass a machine guard, disable a fire system or perform any hazardous work beyond their training. Where a task requires a licensed contractor, professional engineer, certified inspector or industrial hygienist, the text says so.

Regulatory research and verification framework

Who decides what you must do

Most compliance mistakes come from treating every document with a code number as law. Start by sorting what you are reading into one of eight layers. Then check whether that layer binds your building.

Eight-step verification protocol

  1. Name the authority. Which agency issues or enforces it?
  2. Find the primary source. Statute, regulation, ordinance or the standards body itself.
  3. Confirm edition and adoption date. Do not assume the latest edition is in force.
  4. Read the amendments. State and local changes can reverse a model-code rule.
  1. Test scope and exceptions. Construction date, occupancy, type of work, funding source and building class often decide applicability.
  2. Record the date checked and the link, so a reader knows how fresh it is.
  3. Classify the layer from Figure 0.1.
  4. If unverified, say so and send the reader to the AHJ. Never invent a section number.

1.1Opening scenario#

Fictional scenario · Pelican Parish Schools is invented

Dana Whitfield started on Monday. By 6:40 Tuesday morning she had three voicemails about Bayou Crossing Elementary, a 1971 building that was one of fourteen in her new portfolio.

The first was from a teacher. A brown stain had spread across the ceiling of Room 114 over the weekend, and the room smelled damp. The second was from a custodian. The fire-alarm panel by the front office had been beeping for more than a week, so someone had silenced it. The third was from a data-cable contractor, polite and in a hurry, who wanted to cut channels into a plaster wall in the east wing that afternoon.

Three messages, three building systems, three different kinds of risk. Dana had no history with the building, no budget to fix everything and no one to hand the problems to. What she did next would show whether she was managing the facilities or only answering the phone.

This chapter teaches how to think through a morning like that: what you own, what the law expects, what to do first and who to call. Dana’s three problems return throughout the chapter.

After this chapter you should be able to:

  2. List the major responsibility areas of a facility manager and the systems they touch.
  3. Explain the difference between a law, an adopted code, a consensus standard, guidance and district policy.
  4. Decide, for a stated building and task, which requirement layers to check.
  5. Run a first-30-days baseline walk and document what you find in a consistent format.
  6. Rank problems by risk and apply the life-safety override.

1.3Why the subject matters#

Four reasons make the role important beyond keeping the lights on.

  • People. Buildings hold children, patients, residents and workers. Failures of exits, alarms, air and structure fall on them first.
  • Law. Owners and employers carry legal duties. Missed inspections, undocumented repairs and ignored notices become the evidence in a dispute.
  • Money. Deferred maintenance compounds. A small roof repair left alone becomes wet insulation, damaged finishes and a possible mold response. Spending early is usually cheaper, though only a condition assessment can tell you for a given building.
  • Mission. A school cannot teach in a flooded wing, and a plant cannot ship from a locked-out line. Facility work protects the work the building exists to support.

1.4Plain-English technical explanation#

Facility management is the coordinated care of buildings, grounds and their systems so the people inside can do their work safely and the owner’s investment lasts. A useful analogy is a ship’s chief engineer: the vessel is built by someone else, but the chief engineer keeps it running, safe and seaworthy every day until it is retired.

The title overlaps with two others. The table separates them by the question each role asks every day.

In practice they overlap. A school facility manager often acts as owner’s representative on a renovation, and a property manager schedules repairs. The roles differ in what they are accountable for.

Occupants, staff, contractors, inspectors, regulators and board members. You communicate risk to all of them.

Place

Buildings, grounds and systems: structure, envelope, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, fire protection, vertical transportation and site.

Process

Inspections, preventive maintenance, work orders, procurement, emergency response and records.

1.6How to decide whether a requirement applies#

Ask six questions, in order, before you tell anyone something is required.

  1. Who owns and operates the building? Public entity, private non-profit or private for-profit.
  2. What is it used for? Occupancy and activity decide the fire, egress and accessibility rules.
  3. When was it built or last altered? Construction date triggers exemptions and lead-paint rules; alteration triggers current-code duties for the altered area.
  4. What work is happening? Routine maintenance, alteration, demolition and new construction fall under different rules.
  5. What hazards are present? Asbestos, lead, chemicals, hazardous energy, refrigerants, water systems.
  6. Who is paying? Grants, bonds and insurance add contract conditions.

Then use Tool R-1, the Requirement Finder, to list which layers to check, and finish with the eight-step protocol in the framework above.

Verify
The question answers tell you where to look. They do not tell you the final answer. Confirm with the AHJ, counsel or a qualified specialist before acting on a legal conclusion.

1.8Step-by-step inspection demonstration#

Fictional facility
Bayou Crossing Elementary, Pelican Parish Schools. Dana’s baseline walk happens on Tuesday, October 20 (fictional). Each demonstration answers the same eleven questions.

A baseline walk is the first pass through a building you do not know. Its goal is not to fix things. It is to find what could hurt people today, learn what records exist and decide what to do first. Do the walk with a second person, a flashlight, a camera or phone, a notebook or tablet, and a copy of the floor plan.

Demonstration A: The ceiling stain in Room 114
Looking for
Source of water, size and age of the stain, active dripping, sagging tile, visible growth, damp smell.
Why it matters
Water leaks damage finishes and insulation and can support mold growth. A sagging ceiling can also fall.
Documents to review
Roof inspection and repair history, work-order history for the room, roof warranty, drawings of the roof drains above.
Check visually
Stain edges and rings, drip locations, tile sag, damp insulation above a lifted tile (with a stable ladder and two people), signs of condensation on pipes, exterior roof area above from a safe vantage point.
Instruments or specialists
Moisture meter for surfaces; infrared or electronic moisture survey by a roofing or building-envelope specialist; an industrial hygienist if growth or extensive wetting is found.
Potential deficiency
Active roof or plumbing leak, water-damaged ceiling, possible moisture-related growth.
Location and evidence
Room number, grid location (for example, column line C-4), photographs with a scale reference, moisture-meter readings with time, sketch on the floor plan.
Immediate risk
Falling ceiling material and slips are the immediate risks. Cordon the area. Mold risk is evaluated, not assumed.
Who is notified
Principal and teacher, roofing or plumbing contractor, and an industrial hygienist if the extent or growth warrants it.
Prioritize and verify
High, until the source is stopped. Verify by re-inspecting after the next rain and re-reading moisture levels after drying.
Records to keep
Photos, readings, work orders, contractor reports, drying log and re-inspection results.
Demonstration B: The silenced fire-alarm trouble signal
Looking for
The panel display: which trouble condition, how long, who silenced it and what was done.
Why it matters
A trouble condition can mean part of the alarm system will not work in a fire. Silencing the buzzer hides the problem without fixing it.
Documents to review
Panel event history, inspection and testing reports, last service report, impairment log, fire-code requirements as adopted.
Check visually
Panel indicators and message. Do not reset or silence further.
Instruments or specialists
Diagnosis and repair by a licensed fire alarm contractor. Testing follows the adopted fire-alarm standard.
Potential deficiency
Fire-alarm system impairment; missing impairment notification and interim measures.
Location and evidence
Panel location, photo of the display, event log printout, time of discovery.
Immediate risk
High consequence. Treat as critical under the life-safety override until the contractor says the system is working or interim measures are in place.
Who is notified
Principal and superintendent immediately, the alarm contractor, and the fire authority if your adopted code or monitoring contract requires it.
Prioritize and verify
Critical, same day. Verify with a contractor service report and a documented functional test.
Records to keep
Event history, impairment notices, interim-measure log, contractor report, test results.
Demonstration C: The request to cut into a plaster wall
Looking for
Whether the wall is known or suspected to contain asbestos, and what the work would disturb.
Why it matters
Disturbing asbestos-containing material can release fibers. For schools, federal rules require management plans, accredited personnel and specific procedures.
Documents to review
The district’s AHERA management plan, inspection reports and sample results for the building, and the planned scope of cabling work.
Check visually
Wall condition and location relative to mapped homogeneous areas in the plan. Do not touch, sand or sample.
Instruments or specialists
Sampling, assessment and any response action by accredited asbestos professionals. Air monitoring as required.
Potential deficiency
Proposed disturbance of suspect or assumed asbestos-containing material without plan review or notification.
Location and evidence
Wall identifier, copy of the plan page, photo of the wall, contractor’s request in writing.
Immediate risk
Exposure risk if the work starts. Stop-work until verified.
Who is notified
The district’s designated AHERA person, the contractor, and an accredited professional.
Prioritize and verify
High with stop-work. Verify by written clearance, updated plan and documented notification.
Records to keep
Plan excerpts, clearance letter, contractor correspondence, notification copy.
Stop-work conditions
Stop and escalate if you see exposed wiring or live equipment, a suspected hazardous material, an unstable ceiling or roof, a blocked or locked exit, or a closed fire-protection valve. Do not attempt to repair, sample, reset or bypass. Make the area safe, keep people away and call the right specialist.

1.11Realistic case study: The Quiet Panel#

Fictional case
Magnolia Heights Middle School and all people in it are invented. The case is a composite of patterns, not a record of a real event.

What happened. A trouble signal began at the fire-alarm panel in a 1980s middle school. The panel buzzed during classes, so the front-office assistant silenced it. Each morning it buzzed again and was silenced again. A work order was created but never assigned because it was marked “non-urgent, no smoke.” Three weeks later, a contractor working in the ceiling above the cafeteria bumped a detector wire. At the next monthly drill, several horns in the east wing did not sound. The drill was ended and the district called the alarm contractor. The contractor found two separate faults: the original ground fault, and the damaged circuit from the later work.

What went wrong.

  • No one owned the response to a trouble signal. It was treated as noise.
  • The work order had no risk classification, so a life-safety item sat in the same queue as paint touch-ups.
  • No impairment procedure: no interim measures, no notifications, no log.
  • Contractor work above ceilings was not coordinated with the alarm system.

Model answer. A well-run program classifies a trouble signal as critical on day one. The front office has a one-page response: do not silence repeatedly, call the facility manager, who confirms the condition, calls the alarm contractor and, per the adopted fire code and the district’s impairment procedure, notifies the required parties and arranges interim measures. A work order carries a risk field. Contractors are briefed on life-safety systems before ceiling work. The drill found the second fault, but the first could have been fixed three weeks earlier.

Instructor discussion prompts
  1. Which single change would have prevented the most harm?
  2. Who should own the first call when a trouble signal appears at 7:30 a.m.?
  3. How should a work-order system distinguish life-safety items?
  4. What should the contractor-orientation checklist include?

1.13Risk classification and corrective actions#

Risk ranking turns a long list into an order of action. Multiply likelihood (how likely harm is) by consequence (how bad it would be). Then apply the life-safety override: any condition that could prevent occupants from escaping or being warned, or that exposes people to a serious hazard now, is Critical regardless of score.

1.14Common mistakes and professional lessons#

  • Treating a model code as law. It is law only if adopted, in the edition adopted, with local amendments.
  • Fixing the symptom. Painting a stain or silencing an alarm hides the cause.
  • Letting the loudest complaint win. Rank by risk, then explain the order to complainants.
  • Going beyond your qualifications. Opening panels, sampling suspect material or climbing onto a roof alone are classic ways to get hurt.
  • Not writing it down. Undocumented work cannot be shown to an auditor or defended later.
  • Promising what you cannot do. Policies stricter than your capacity become liabilities.
  • Skipping contractor orientation. Contractors damage life-safety systems and disturb hazardous materials when no one tells them what is above the ceiling.

    Ownership, Operations, Maintenance and Lifecycle

    Why do buildings that look fine fail suddenly, and what does it take to keep that from happening?

    2.1Opening scenario#

    Fictional scenario · Cypress Middle School is invented

    On the Tuesday before school opened in August, Dana Whitfield stood on the roof of Cypress Middle with a technician and watched a rooftop air conditioner shudder to a stop. Inside, the gym was already 86 degrees and the first teacher in-service was Thursday.

    The technician replaced the drive belt for the third time since March. “It keeps eating belts,” he said. He had never been told why. The filter rack was packed with dust, a condensate line was half blocked, and the logbook taped inside the panel door ended in April.

    Back in the office, the work-order system showed forty-one calls for that single unit in two years, almost all marked “complete.” Not one was a scheduled visit. Dana realized the building had never been maintained. It had only been repaired.

    This chapter explains the difference, and how to move a portfolio from the first to the second without a bigger budget.

    2.2Learning objectives#

    1. Describe the phases of a building’s life and who owns the decisions in each.
    2. Distinguish preventive, predictive, corrective and reactive maintenance, and say when each makes sense.
    3. Explain why some maintenance is a legal or contractual duty and not a choice.
    4. Compare maintenance strategies using a simple expected-cost model.
    5. Audit a preventive-maintenance program using records and a short walk.
    6. Choose a strategy for an asset from its criticality and failure behavior.

    2.3Why the subject matters#

    • Failures cluster at the worst time. Heating fails in the cold snap and cooling fails in the heat wave, because that is when systems work hardest.
    • Emergencies cost more than plans. Rush labor, expedited parts and rental equipment usually cost more than the same work scheduled. How much more depends on the asset and the market, which is why you measure your own.
    • Some maintenance is required. Fire protection, pressure vessels, elevators and backflow devices often carry inspection duties set by law, adopted codes or insurers.
    • Warranties and insurance can depend on it. Many equipment warranties require documented service. Missing records can void them.
    • Lifecycle decisions are hard to reverse. Choices made at design and purchase set what the building will cost to operate for decades.

    2.6How to decide whether a requirement applies#

    2.11Realistic case study: The January Boiler#

    Fictional case
    Westbank Annex and the people in it are invented. The case is a composite of patterns, not a record of a real event.

    What happened. Westbank Annex ran on two 1990s boilers. For three years the annual boiler service was deferred to save money, and one boiler had been shut down in each of the last two heating seasons for a “minor leak.” In the first week of January a cold snap arrived. The remaining boiler tripped on low water, and the technician found that a feedwater valve and the water-treatment program had both been neglected. Scale in the unit had been building for years. With temperatures near freezing, the school closed for three days while a rental boiler was craned onto the roof. Pipes froze in one wing. The district paid rental, rigging, emergency labor, plumbing repairs and lost instructional days.

    Root causes.

    • Service was cut first because it left no visible effect.
    • Equipment with redundancy was left with a single working unit and no one noticed.
    • The state inspection certificate was current, so leadership assumed the plant was sound. A certificate shows a legal minimum, not reliability.
    • No condition data was reported to the budget committee.

    Model answer. Treat boilers as critical assets with preventive service and water treatment that cannot be deferred without a documented risk decision. Track redundancy as a status: “N+1 available” or “no spare.” Report to the board when redundancy is lost. Present the cost of deferral in dollars and in days of closure.

    2. How would you present the cost of the deferred service so a board would act?

      3.1Opening scenario#

      Fictional scenario · Magnolia Annex and Pelican Parish Schools are invented

      3.3Why the subject matters#

      • Budgets are won with evidence. A board funds a number with a method behind it faster than it funds a complaint.
      • Failures hide in unlisted assets. The fire pump nobody counted is the one nobody tests.
      • Insurance, grants and audits ask. Lenders, insurers and funders often want inventories and condition information. What each requires depends on the contract or program, so read it.
      • Capital planning needs a baseline. You cannot plan renewal of equipment you cannot age.
      • Comparisons need one yardstick. Without a common rating scale, every building looks worst to the person who works there.

      3.4Plain-English technical explanation#

      What an asset register is

      An asset register (or inventory) is a list of the things you maintain, with enough information on each to find it, identify it, age it, value it and schedule its care. It is a living record, not a one-time project.

      Hierarchy

      Group assets so they can be reported at different levels. A common structure runs site, building, floor or area, system, component. The same rooftop unit then rolls up to the roof system, the HVAC system, the building and the portfolio, depending on who is asking.

      Condition assessment

      A condition assessment is a structured walk and records review that grades each system or asset on a fixed scale and estimates the work needed to bring it back to an acceptable state. A property condition assessment done for a purchase or loan follows an agreed scope, often built on a consensus guide such as ASTM E2018. Check the edition and the scope your lender or owner actually requires.

      The Facility Condition Index

      FCI compares need to value:

      Formula
      FCI = deferred maintenance (renewal need) ÷ current replacement value. A building with $2.4 million of identified need and a $12 million replacement value has an FCI of 0.20, or 20%.

      Organizations define the numerator differently (some count only deferred maintenance, others add near-term renewals), and the rating bands also vary. Chapter 1 used the common teaching bands of under 5, 5 to 10, 10 to 30 and over 30 percent. Treat any published band as a convention. Always state your definition when you quote an FCI.

      Limit
      FCI is a financial ratio. It does not say whether a building is safe, and a large new building can look better than a small old one for the wrong reason. Rank life-safety deficiencies by risk, not by FCI.

        When everything is urgent, how do you decide what happens first, and defend the order?

        Chapter 4 of 20

        4.1Opening scenario#

        Fictional scenario · Harbor Point High School and its people are invented

        On Friday afternoon Renee Castillo, facilities director at Harbor Point High, had fifteen open items on her desk and a crew of four. The principal wanted the gym floor refinished before homecoming. The band director wanted the practice-room air conditioner fixed.         This chapter gives you that formula, the rule that sits above it, and a way to write down the reasoning so an auditor or board member can follow it.

        4.2Learning objectives#

        1. Score a deficiency by likelihood and consequence on a defined scale.
        2. Apply the life-safety override and explain why it sits above any score.
        3. Name the other factors that raise priority: overdue mandatory duties, exposure, redundancy and cost of delay.
        4. Recognize what an imminent-danger condition is and who must be told.
        5. Rank a mixed list of deficiencies and defend the order in writing.
        6. Avoid the common biases: loudest voice, easiest fix, newest item.

        4.3Why the subject matters#

        • Resources are finite. Every list is longer than the crew and the budget.
        • The squeaky wheel is not the risk. Complaints measure annoyance. Risk measures harm.
        • A recorded decision protects people and the organization. If a dangerous item was known and left, the record shows who knew what, when and what they did.
        • Consistency builds trust. Staff accept an order they can see the reasons for.
        • Money follows ranked evidence. A ranked list with a method is a budget request that survives questions.

        4.4Plain-English technical explanation#

        The basic score

        Risk score = likelihood × consequence. Both use a 1 to 5 scale with written definitions.

        ScoreLikelihood (that harm or failure occurs)Consequence (if it does)
        1Rare: not expected in the life of the assetNegligible: nuisance, no injury, no lost use
        2Unlikely: could happen once in several yearsMinor: first aid, short inconvenience
        3Possible: could happen within a yearModerate: medical treatment, a room or system out of use for days
        4Likely: has happened here or will probably happen this yearMajor: serious injury, a building area closed, large cost
        5Almost certain: happening now or expected within weeksSevere: death or life-changing injury, school or building closure, regulatory action

        Bands used in this book, as in Chapter 1: Critical at 20 or more, High at 10 to 19, Medium at 5 to 9, Low below 5. They are illustrative. Adopt your own organization’s bands if it has them and keep them stable over time.

        The life-safety override

        Rule
        Any condition that could keep people from detecting a fire, getting out, or being protected from fire or other life-threatening hazard is rated Critical regardless of its score, until it is made safe. A chained exit door, a closed sprinkler control valve, a silenced alarm and a blocked egress path all qualify.

        Adjusters that raise or lower priority

        AdjusterQuestionEffect
        Mandatory duty overdueIs a required inspection, test or certificate past due?Raise one band. Past-due pressure-vessel or fire-protection work is not Low.
        ExposureHow many people, and how often, are exposed?A hazard in a gym during events outranks the same hazard in a seldom-used storage yard.
        RedundancyIs there a working backup?Loss of the only boiler is worse than loss of one of two working boilers.
        Cost of delayDoes the damage grow if left, for example a roof leak?Raise priority when delay multiplies the cost.
        Quick, cheap fixCan it be fixed today for little cost?Do it now. Cheap fixes should not wait for ranking.

        A source of potential harm: a trip edge, an energized part, a blocked exit.

        The combination of how likely harm is and how bad it would be.

        A condition expected to cause death or serious harm immediately. Remove people first.

          Budgets, OpEx, CapEx and Lifecycle Cost

          Why does the cheapest bid so often cost the most, and how do you show that to the people holding the money?

          Chapter 5 of 20

          5.1Opening scenario#

          Fictional scenario · Lakeview Charter Academy and its people are invented

          Tamika Reed, operations director at Lakeview Charter Academy, had two roof quotes on her desk. The first was $410,000 for a roof with a 15-year life. The second was $560,000 for one with a 30-year life. The board chair had already circled the first number. “We are $150,000 apart. Why would we pay more?”

          Tamika did not answer with an opinion. She went home and built a table: the cost of each roof today, the yearly upkeep, the replacement at year 15 for the short-lived roof, and what a dollar later is worth compared with a dollar now. By the next meeting she could say that over thirty years, the cheaper roof would cost the school more.

          The board still had to decide how to pay for it. Roofs are not operating expenses, and the school’s budget had no line for them. That was the second conversation, and it was harder.

          This chapter covers both: how to compare options over their life, and how to build a budget that has a place for renewal.

          5.2Learning objectives#

          1. Distinguish operating expense (OpEx) from capital expense (CapEx) and say who decides which is which.
          2. Explain lifecycle cost and total cost of ownership.
          3. Compute a present-value comparison of two options over a study period.
          4. Estimate an annual renewal reserve from replacement cost and useful life.
          5. Build a facility budget with operating, reserve and contingency lines.
          6. Write a budget request that a board or auditor can follow.

          5.3Why the subject matters#

          • Low bids hide high costs. Purchase price is often a minor share of what an asset costs over its life.
          • Deferred maintenance is borrowing. The cost does not vanish. It returns later, usually larger and at a worse time.
          • Budget categories steer behavior. If there is no renewal line, renewal is skipped until failure.
          • Public and grant money has rules. Procurement and appropriation law, bond terms and grant conditions limit what you can buy and how.
          • Boards need a number with a method. A structured comparison turns an argument into a decision.

          5.4Plain-English technical explanation#

          Operating versus capital

          Operating expense (OpEx)Capital expense (CapEx)
          Typical itemsUtilities, custodial supplies, routine maintenance, minor repairs, service contractsRoof replacement, new boiler, major renovation, new building
          RhythmEvery yearIrregular, large
          Usual fundingAnnual operating budgetCapital fund, bonds, reserves, grants
          Who sets the lineYour finance office and auditor, through a written capitalization policy. The facility manager follows it and does not invent it.
          Caution
          The same dollar can be OpEx in one organization and CapEx in another, depending on the capitalization threshold and policy. Ask your finance office for the written policy before you label a request.

          Lifecycle cost

          Lifecycle cost (or total cost of ownership) adds up everything an asset costs over a chosen study period: purchase and installation, operation, maintenance, repair, replacement at end of life, and any residual value at the end.

          Time value of money

          A dollar next year is worth less than a dollar today. Present value converts future costs into today’s dollars using a discount rate. If the discount rate is 4 percent, $1,000 spent in a year costs about $962 today. Escalation (cost inflation) works in the opposite direction. This chapter uses a simple model with both, and says plainly when a number is an assumption.

          Formulas
          Present value of a future cost = cost ÷ (1 + r)t for a one-time cost at year t. For an annual cost, add the present value of each year. Annual renewal reserve ≈ replacement cost ÷ useful life. A roof that costs $560,000 and lasts 30 years needs about $18,700 set aside every year to pay for itself.

          The reserve idea

          If every system in a building will need replacement someday, the budget should set money aside each year instead of waiting for a surprise. Summed across systems, that annual amount is a rough renewal reserve. Compare it with what you actually spend to see the gap.

          What a lifecycle comparison would have shown. Over a 30-year period, the cheaper roof needed two replacements and more upkeep. The premium option cost about a third more at the start and less over 30 years under most reasonable assumptions.

          What went wrong. The specification did not require a minimum life or a maintenance plan, and nobody compared total cost. The budget had no reserve, so the replacement landed on a project that was ready to go.

          Lessons. (1) Write minimum life and warranty terms into the specification, within procurement rules. (2) Compare total cost, not price. (3) Fund renewal on a schedule, not on failure.

            You are paying for the work. How do you know you got it?

            Chapter 6 of 20

            6.1Opening scenario#

            Fictional scenario · Riverbend Academy and Gulf Coast Mechanical are invented

            Luis Armand took over facilities at Riverbend Academy in August. In his first week he found a folder labeled “HVAC contract.” It held a one-page agreement that said Gulf Coast Mechanical would “service the heating and cooling equipment as needed” for a flat monthly fee. It listed no equipment, no visit schedule, no response time and no reports.

            The invoices came every month. The visit records came never. When a chiller failed in September, the vendor arrived two days later and billed the repair as extra. Luis asked for service reports and was told, politely, that none were required.

            The contract was not a fraud. It was a blank check with a signature on it.

            This chapter is about writing and managing agreements so that paying for work and getting work are the same thing.

            6.2Learning objectives#

            1. Write a scope of work that names the equipment, tasks, frequency and deliverables.
            2. Include response times, reports and acceptance criteria in a service agreement.
            3. Check a contractor’s license, insurance and authority before work starts.
            4. Track warranties so claims are made in time.
            5. Score vendor performance with a consistent method.
            6. Spot weak clauses in an agreement and know when to ask a lawyer.

            6.3Why the subject matters#

            • Most building work is outsourced. Fire protection, elevators, HVAC, roofing and many other systems depend on vendors.
            • A vague contract cannot be enforced. If it does not say what, when and how well, there is nothing to hold anyone to.
            • Required inspections are done by vendors. If the vendor skips a test, the owner still carries the duty.
            • Warranties expire quietly. A defect found on day 366 of a one-year warranty is the owner’s cost.
            • Insurance and licensing protect you. An unlicensed or uninsured contractor moves risk to the owner.

            What a good service agreement states

            1. Scope: which equipment (by tag or list), which tasks, and the standard each follows (manufacturer instructions or an adopted code).
            2. Frequency and schedule: how often, in what window, with advance notice.
            3. Response times: for emergencies, urgent and routine calls, with how they are measured.

            7. Pricing: fixed fee, rate sheet, parts markup, what is excluded.
            8. Term and renewal: start, end, whether it renews automatically, notice to cancel.
            9. Acceptance and remedies: how work is accepted, what happens if it is not done.
            10. Records: who owns them and how you get them if the vendor leaves.

            Verification and oversight

            A contract is a promise. Verification is how you find out whether it is kept: read the reports, spot-check the work, compare invoices with visits and keep a scorecard. Do not pay for work you cannot show was done.

                Part II · Understanding Building Systems

                Structural Systems, Foundations, Slabs and Roofs

                A building is a set of paths that carry weight to the ground. What does it look like when one of them is in trouble, and what are you allowed to do about it?

                Chapter 8 of 20

                Safety boundary for this chapter
                A facility manager observes, records, monitors and escalates. A licensed structural engineer diagnoses causes and decides on repairs, shoring, load limits and reoccupancy. Do not cut, drill, remove or add load to structural members, and do not enter a space you suspect is unstable. If you see sudden movement, new loud cracking, a sagging ceiling or a door that has jammed overnight, clear the area and call for help.

                8.1Opening scenario#

                Fictional scenario · Marsh Landing Elementary and its people are invented

                A second-grade teacher told Carlos Ibarra, the custodial supervisor at Marsh Landing Elementary, that her classroom door had started sticking. By Friday it needed a shoulder to close. Carlos shaved the door edge with a plane, which fixed the sticking and hid the message.

                In October, a student pointed out a crack that ran diagonally from the top corner of the same door frame. Carlos photographed it, put a pencil mark and date at each end, and sent the photo to the facility manager, Janelle Wheeler. She walked the wing the same afternoon. She noticed a downspout that emptied against the foundation, a flower bed that held water, and a second diagonal crack at the next window.

                She did not guess at the cause. She cordoned off nothing, because nothing suggested immediate danger, but she posted a notice to staff to report any new cracks or sudden movement, and called a structural engineer the same day.

                The engineer found settlement from soil that had been repeatedly saturated by the downspout discharge. Fixing the drainage came first. The cracks were then monitored. This chapter teaches Carlos’s and Janelle’s part of the story: what to notice, how to record it and when to call.

                8.2Learning objectives#

                1. Trace a load path from roof to soil and say what each element does.
                2. Name the common structural systems found in schools and commercial buildings.
                3. Describe the warning signs of settlement, overload, corrosion, moisture and fire damage.
                4. Record cracks and movement so that an engineer can use the record.
                5. Know the line between observation and engineering judgment.
                6. Escalate correctly and follow up.

                8.3Why the subject matters#

                • Structure is the one system you cannot afford to lose. A failed pump can be replaced. A failed beam can hurt people.
                • Most structural trouble gives warning. Cracks, sticking doors, sagging, rust stains and water are early signals if someone notices and writes them down.
                • The cause is often water. Roof, drainage and plumbing leaks cause or speed up many structural problems.
                • Alterations change loads. Heavy equipment, new roof layers, removed walls and stored materials can overload a structure that was fine before.
                • Records matter. A dated photograph with a scale is worth more to an engineer than a description from memory.

                8.4Plain-English technical explanation#

                The load path

                Every load, from snow and wind to students and equipment, must travel to the ground through a continuous chain: roof covering and deck, joists or trusses, beams, columns or bearing walls, foundations, soil. If any link is weakened, loads shift to its neighbors. Cracks and deflection show where the chain is strained.

                Cracks are the building’s way of showing strain.                 4. Date, time, weather in the last few days, and who observed.
                5. Pencil marks at the crack ends with dates, or a crack gauge fixed across it.
                6. Associated signs: sticking doors, gaps at windows, sloping floors, water.

                8.6How to decide whether a requirement applies#

                1. Is there any sign of imminent danger? Sudden movement, new loud cracking, sagging, collapse of any part. Clear the area, call for help and follow Chapter 4.

                8.7Building systems, components and terminology#

                Load path

                The chain of elements that carry weight from the roof to the ground.

                Dead load / live load

                Dead load is the building’s own weight. Live load is people, furniture, equipment and stored items. Snow and wind are separate.

                Footing

                The wide base under a wall or column that spreads load into soil.

                Slab-on-grade

                A concrete floor poured directly on prepared ground.

                Settlement

                Downward movement of a foundation as soil compresses. Differential settlement, where one part moves more than another, causes cracks.

                Heave

                Upward movement, often from swelling clay soils or frost.

                Deflection

                Sagging of a beam, joist or slab under load.

                Spalling

                Flaking or breaking away of concrete surface, often from corroding rebar.

                Efflorescence

                White salty deposit left when water moves through masonry or concrete. A sign of moisture.

                Shoring

                Temporary support installed to carry load. Designed by an engineer.

                    Building Envelope, Roofs, Windows, Doors and Drainage

                    Water is patient. How do you keep it out, and how do you trace it when it gets in?

                    Chapter 9 of 20

                    Safety boundary for this chapter
                    Roofs are fall hazards. Do not walk a roof, lean over an edge or step near a skylight without a written access plan, the right fall protection and a second person. The default for this book is to inspect from the ground and from the interior, and to use a qualified roofing contractor for the roof itself. Never test a skylight or a ceiling by stepping or pressing on it. Wet, icy, windy or stormy conditions are never a reason to go up.

                    9.1Opening scenario#

                    Fictional scenario · Bayou Pines Elementary and its people are invented

                    The first heavy rain of fall brought a wet ceiling tile in the library and a dripping sound in the hallway outside it. The custodian, Reggie, put a bucket under the drip, wrote “leak, library” on the whiteboard and went back to work. The bucket was empty by lunch. The leak, everyone assumed, was over.

                    Three weeks later, a second storm brought it back, with a new stain two ceiling tiles over. The facility manager, Tomasz, asked a simple question: where was the water coming from? Looking at the ceiling would not tell him, because water travels. He checked, in order: the roof above, the nearest roof drain, the flashing at a rooftop unit curb, the wall that met the roof at that corner, and the window head outside the library.

                    It was the flashing at the curb, three meters from the stain. A quick sealant fix had been applied years earlier, and it had cracked. Tomasz did not go on the roof. He took photographs from the second-floor window, wrote a scope and had the roofing contractor make the repair with a safe-access plan.

                    This chapter is about reading water, protecting the layers that keep it out, and doing it without anyone falling.

                    9.2Learning objectives#

                    1. Name the layers of a roof and a wall that control water, air and heat.
                    2. Describe common roof types and their typical failure points.
                    3. Trace a leak from the stain back to its likely source.
                    4. Inspect windows, doors, sealants and drainage from safe positions.
                    5. Plan a roof access decision using the fall-protection rules in this book.
                    6. Write a repair scope that a contractor can price and a manager can verify.

                    9.3Why the subject matters#

                    • Water is the most common cause of building damage. It rots wood, rusts steel, grows mold, ruins ceilings and equipment and weakens structure (Chapter 8).
                    • Roofs are expensive and short on warning. A small leak can mean wet insulation across a large area.
                    • Envelope problems drive energy and comfort complaints. Drafts, condensation and hot rooms often start at the envelope.
                    • Roof work is dangerous. Falls are a leading cause of serious injury in construction and maintenance.
                    • Small, cheap maintenance prevents big repairs. Clearing drains and resealing a joint cost far less than replacing a roof early.

                    9.4Plain-English technical explanation#

                      Fictional case

                        Part II · Understanding Building Systems

                        HVAC, Ventilation, Filtration, Refrigeration and Controls

                        Most complaints about a building are really complaints about air. How do you work out what the air system is doing, and where to stop?

                        Chapter 10 of 20

                        Safety boundary for this chapter
                        Observe, measure from safe positions, and call qualified technicians. Do not open electrical disconnects or panels, remove fan guards or belt covers on running equipment, handle refrigerant or open refrigerant circuits, bypass safety controls or work on gas-fired equipment. Refrigerant work requires an EPA-certified technician. Never go on a roof without the access plan in Chapter 9. If you smell gas, leave the area and call the utility and emergency services.

                        10.1Opening scenario#

                        Fictional scenario · Willow Run Middle School and its people are invented

                        In September, three teachers at Willow Run Middle School separately reported the same thing: “the kids are drowsy by third period.” Room 207 was hot. Room 209, next door, was cold. The principal wanted the thermostats lowered. The HVAC contractor suggested a larger unit.

                        Facility manager Imani Okafor asked a smaller question: what does the air actually look like in these rooms? She borrowed a CO2 meter from the district office. At the end of third period, Room 207 read well above the outdoor reading. She looked at the rooftop unit schedule on the control screen. The outdoor-air damper was set to its minimum position and the unit was running on a schedule that started only fifteen minutes before school. A filter bank was nearly black.

                        The fix was not a bigger unit. It was a filter change, a corrected damper setting and a schedule that began earlier. The hot and cold rooms turned out to be a stuck damper actuator and a thermostat on the wrong wall.

                        This chapter teaches how to read an air system from the outside, so that the right specialist is called for the right reason.

                        10.2Learning objectives#

                        1. Describe the path of air through a typical air-handling system and the job of each component.
                        2. Explain what ventilation, filtration, heating, cooling and controls each do.
                        3. Describe the refrigeration cycle in plain language and the legal limits on refrigerant handling.
                        4. Estimate ventilation per person from a carbon dioxide reading and state the limits of the method.
                        5. Diagnose common comfort complaints in the right order.
                        6. Know when to call a technician, an air-balance specialist or an engineer.

                        10.3Why the subject matters#

                        • HVAC is usually the largest energy user and the largest source of complaints.
                        • Air quality affects people. Ventilation, filtration and humidity influence comfort and how people feel and perform.
                        • Failures are expensive and seasonal. Chillers and boilers fail on the hottest and coldest days (Chapter 2).
                        • Refrigerants are regulated. Handling them without certification is a legal problem and an environmental one.
                        • Controls hide problems. A system on a wrong schedule or with a stuck damper can waste energy for years without a single breakdown.

                        10.4Plain-English technical explanation#

                        The air path

                        Most buildings move air through the same sequence: outdoor air and return air mix; the mixture passes through a filter; a coil heats or cools it; a fan pushes it through ducts to supply diffusers in rooms; return air goes back through grilles and ducts; some air is exhausted and replaced with outdoor air.                         The refrigeration cycle, briefly

                        Cooling equipment moves heat from inside to outside using a refrigerant that changes between liquid and gas. A compressor pressurizes the refrigerant gas. In the condenser it gives up heat and becomes a liquid. A metering device drops its pressure. In the evaporator coil it absorbs heat from the air passing over it and becomes a gas again. The cycle repeats. Anything that cuts airflow (dirty filter, dirty coil, failed fan) or changes the charge changes how well this works.

                        Limit
                        Refrigerant charge, pressures and electrical measurements are for certified technicians. A facility manager can see the symptoms, such as ice on the coil, oily residue at fittings, short cycling, warm supply air, and report them with accurate records.

                        Ventilation and a carbon dioxide reading

                        People breathe out carbon dioxide (CO2). In an occupied room, the indoor CO2 reading above the outdoor reading is a rough indicator of how much outdoor air each person is getting. It is not a measure of air pollutants and it does not replace a ventilation measurement. It is useful for a quick screen, with a calibrated meter, after the room has been occupied long enough for the reading to settle. Tool 10.1 uses the steady-state idea.

                        Humidity and filtration

                        Too humid air feeds mold and feels clammy. Very dry air is uncomfortable. Filters are graded with a MERV number from 1 to 16: a higher number catches smaller particles but resists airflow more. A unit designed for one grade may not move enough air through a denser filter. Ask the manufacturer or an HVAC technician before changing grade.

                        10.6How to decide whether a requirement applies#

                        1. Is there a risk to people right now? Gas smell, carbon monoxide alarm, smoke, flooding, loss of heat in extreme cold. Evacuate or protect people, then call. Follow Chapter 4.
                        2. Does the work involve refrigerant? Only certified technicians. Venting is prohibited.
                        3. Is it a boiler or a pressure vessel? State inspection rules and a qualified technician.
                        4. Is it a change to the system (new equipment, new ductwork, new capacity)? Codes, permits and an engineer probably apply.
                        5. Is it ventilation or air quality? Find out which code or standard is adopted and what the design basis was.
                        6. Otherwise follow manufacturer instructions and the strategy chosen in Chapter 2.

                        10.7Building systems, components and terminology#

                        Air handling unit (AHU)

                        A box with fan, coils and filters that conditions and moves air.

                        Rooftop unit (RTU)

                        A self-contained unit on the roof that heats, cools and ventilates.

                        VAV box

                        Variable air volume terminal that changes the amount of air to a room.

                        Economizer

                        Uses cool outdoor air for free cooling when conditions allow.

                        Chiller and boiler

                        Central equipment that makes chilled or hot water for coils.

                        BAS / BMS

                        Building automation system that schedules and controls equipment.

                        MERV

                        Minimum efficiency reporting value, a grade for filters.

                        Pressure relationship

                        Whether a space is positive or negative to its surroundings. It affects infiltration.

                        Test and balance (TAB)

                        Measuring and adjusting airflows and water flows to design values.

                        Setpoint and schedule

                        The target temperature and the hours the system runs.
