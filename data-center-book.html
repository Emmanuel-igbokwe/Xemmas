Chapter 1

THE DATA CENTER BUSINESS, THE PROJECT TEAM AND THE STAKEHOLDERS

Who is involved, what do they want, and how is the project organized?

15 min read · 3,238 words

Big ideaThe big idea#

A data center is a machine that converts electrical power into reliable computing, housed in a building. Everything about the construction project follows from that: the building is mostly power and cooling equipment, the customer pays for megawatts available on a specific date, and a delay is measured in lost revenue per day. If you understand who is paying for what, who controls the power, and who can stop the work, you understand most of what a project manager does.

1.1Why data center projects are different#

A typical office building is mostly architecture with building systems inside it. A data center inverts that. On Delta Ridge, roughly two thirds of the construction cost is electrical and mechanical equipment and installation, and the shell that houses it is a comparatively simple, repetitive structure. That has consequences you will feel every day.

Feature What it means for the project
Speed to market The owner's tenants (or the owner's own business) need capacity by a committed date. Time is the most valuable resource, and schedule pressure is constant
Power is the critical resource The utility connection, substation and large electrical equipment often decide the finish date, not the building
Long-lead equipment Generators, transformers, switchgear, UPS systems and chillers can take many months to a few years to deliver. Procurement is as important as construction
Repetition Four identical data halls means learning curves, prefabrication and standardized details pay off enormously
Zero tolerance for failure The facility must run for years without a power or cooling interruption. Quality, testing and commissioning carry unusual weight
Phased delivery Capacity is turned over hall by hall while construction continues next door, so safety and separation between live and under-construction areas matter
Owner-furnished equipment and tenant fit-out Servers, racks, networking and sometimes major equipment come from the owner or the tenant, so coordination of who delivers what, and when, is a core task
Scrutiny from outside Communities, utilities and regulators care about power demand, water use, noise, emissions and tax incentives

The type of owner shapes how the project behaves.

Delta Ridge is a wholesale colocation project for an anchor tenant: four data halls of 8 MW each, with the first hall committed for service about 15 months after notice to proceed.

1.3The vocabulary you must know on day one#

  • IT load (critical load): the power drawn by the servers and networking, measured in megawatts (MW). Delta Ridge has 32 MW of IT capacity.

  • White space: the data hall floor where racks sit. Gray space: the electrical rooms, mechanical rooms and support areas.

  • Redundancy: extra equipment so that a failure or maintenance does not interrupt service. N means exactly the capacity needed. N+1 adds one spare unit. 2N means two completely independent systems, each able to carry the full load.

  • Concurrent maintainability: any component can be maintained or replaced without shutting down the IT load. (Uptime Institute's Tier III is the widely used reference for this concept; always check which standard or certification your contract requires.)

  • PUE (power usage effectiveness): a measure of efficiency.

PUE = Total facility energy ÷ IT equipment energy

A PUE of 1.3 means that for every 1.0 unit of energy used by servers, 0.3 more is used by cooling, power losses and lighting. Delta Ridge's design target is about 1.3, so a 32 MW IT load implies a peak facility load of roughly 42 MW.

  • RFS (ready for service): the contractual milestone when a hall is complete, tested and handed to the customer.

  • OFCI / OFOI: owner-furnished, contractor-installed; owner-furnished, owner-installed equipment.

  • SLA (service level agreement): the uptime promise the owner makes to customers; it is why quality and testing matter so much.

Inside a general contractor's data center team

  • Project executive / director: owns the owner relationship and the project's profit across the portfolio.

  • Project manager (PM): contract, budget, subcontracts, owner communication, change orders, billing. This is the role this book prepares you for.

  • Superintendent(s): run the field day to day, with a general superintendent over a large campus and assistant superintendents for each area.

  • MEP manager or electrical and mechanical leads: coordinate the largest trades, since they hold most of the cost and risk.

  • Procurement manager and expediters: track long-lead equipment from order through factory to site.

  • Project engineers and assistant PMs: RFIs, submittals, change documents, pay applications.

  • Commissioning and energization lead: plans testing and the safe energization of electrical systems.

  • Quality, safety, scheduling and BIM / VDC managers: specialists supporting the team.

The PM and superintendent pair works the same way as on any project: the PM controls paper, money and relationships; the superintendent controls the site. On a data center there is a third essential partner: the MEP manager, because the electrical and mechanical trades decide whether the project succeeds.

1.5How data center projects are delivered#

Method How it works Where it fits data centers
Design-bid-build Owner completes design, then bids to contractors Rare for large data centers; too slow and offers little early input
Design-build One entity designs and builds under a single contract Common for repeatable designs and speed
Construction manager at risk (CMAR) with GMP The CM joins during design, advises on cost and schedule, then commits to a guaranteed maximum price Very common; allows early release packages (site work, long-lead equipment) before full design is complete
EPC / turnkey A contractor takes responsibility for engineering, procurement and construction for a fixed price Used when the owner wants a single point of responsibility, including equipment supply
Owner-led with trade partners The owner buys major equipment directly and assigns it to the contractor to install Common on hyperscale programs to secure long-lead items early

Delta Ridge uses CMAR with early release packages: site and utility work starts under an early works agreement; major electrical and mechanical equipment is ordered under early procurement packages; the GMP is set at $400,000,000 once design reaches about 60 percent. Chapter 3 shows how that works.

The contract chain

Money and responsibility follow contracts, not friendships. The owner contracts with the designers and with the CM/GC. The GC contracts with subcontractors and, in many cases, directly with equipment manufacturers. The utility has its own agreement with the owner. You usually have no contract with parties two steps away, so you communicate through the party you are contracted with.

1.6The lifecycle of a data center project#

  1. Business case and site selection: power availability, land, fiber, water, climate, incentives, community.

  2. Utility engagement: capacity study, interconnection agreement, substation design. Often the longest-running activity.

  3. Entitlements and permits: zoning, environmental, air permits for generators, building permits.

  4. Design: concept, schematic, design development, construction documents, with early packages released along the way.

  5. Preconstruction: estimating, constructability, procurement strategy, schedule, GMP (Chapter 3).

  6. Procurement and award: subcontract buyout, equipment orders (Chapter 4).

  7. Mobilization and site work: clearing, utilities, foundations.

  8. Shell and core construction: structure, roof, envelope.

  9. MEP installation and fit-out: power, cooling, fire, security, controls (Chapter 7).

  10. Commissioning, integrated testing and turnover (Chapter 9), then ramp-up, warranty and operations.

1.7The stakeholder map#

A project manager has responsibility without authority over most of the people who decide whether the project succeeds. You cannot order the utility, the inspector, the supplier or another company's foreman. You can only influence them. The first step is to map who matters.

Place every stakeholder by power (can they stop, delay or approve work?) and interest (how closely do they watch and care?). Notice that on a data center the utility company sits in the top-right quadrant: its power, and its schedule, decide your critical path. 1.8Stakeholder intervention scenarios#

SCENARIO 1The anchor tenant asks to move Hall 1's date forward by six weeks

The tenant has a business reason, and it reaches you through the owner. The schedule has little float, and the utility energization date is uncertain.

  1. Do not say no or yes in the meeting. Say you will return with options within three working days.

  2. Rebuild the critical path with the superintendent and scheduler: what drives Hall 1 (utility energization, generator delivery, commissioning)?

  3. Price options: overtime and second shifts, prefabricated electrical rooms, extra crews for cable pulling, extra commissioning teams, early shipping of equipment. Example: a package of acceleration measures might cost $6.8 million to recover six weeks.

  4. Put the cost of delay next to it. If Hall 1 is worth about $1.2 million of revenue per month to the owner, six weeks is worth roughly $1.8 million, so the acceleration costs more than it earns unless tenant penalties or strategic value justify it.

  5. Return with a choice: the owner may accept a partial acceleration (for example, three weeks for $2.1 million) or choose to renegotiate the tenant date.

  6. Document the decision and the funding source in the minutes.

Influence principle: people accept constraints when they see a quantified menu and feel they chose among options.

SCENARIO 2The utility says the substation energization will slip eight weeks

  1. Verify the facts: which activities are behind and why? Is it equipment (a transformer), permitting, or their crews?

  2. Escalate through the owner to the utility's account executive; most large owners have a relationship there. Bring a one-page schedule showing the impact in days and dollars.

  3. Look for bridging options: temporary generation to start commissioning on generators, early energization of part of the system, phased load acceptance.

  4. Check the contract: is utility delay an excusable, compensable or non-compensable event? Send written notice within the required time.

  5. Protect your side: resequence work that can proceed, redeploy crews, and keep a record of idle time.

SCENARIO 3A community group objects to generator noise and truck traffic

  1. Meet them early, at their location if possible, with the superintendent and the owner's community liaison.

  2. Bring data: truck routes, hours, noise measurements, the air permit's operating limits for testing generators.

  3. Offer practical changes: quieter equipment, delivery windows, a named hotline, a monthly update meeting, a third-party noise monitor.

  4. Record commitments and honor them visibly.

Influence principle: people who feel heard and respected escalate far less often. A courteous, quick response costs little compared with a stop-work order.

1.9A field story (composite)#

A new project engineer is asked to chase the status of the 138 kV transformer for the substation. She calls the manufacturer's sales contact, who gives a friendly, vague update. Her PM suggests a better approach: request the factory's actual production schedule with the next three test dates, ask who at the utility is monitoring it, and add those dates to the master schedule. Two weeks later, the factory reveals that a winding subcontractor is behind; because the team knew in March, not in August, they used the time to arrange a temporary power strategy for early commissioning. Knowing the real stakeholder in the chain, and the real date, turned a surprise into a managed risk.

1.10Common mistakes#

  • Treating the data center as "just a warehouse" and underestimating the electrical and mechanical complexity.

  • Ignoring the utility until it becomes the problem.

  • Giving direction to another firm's workers, which can transfer responsibility and cost to you.

  • Letting the owner's tenant instructions bypass the contract's change process.

  • Assuming every stakeholder wants the same thing.

1.11Your first moves#

  1. Draw the contract chain for your project on one page, with names and phone numbers.

  2. Build a stakeholder map and register with at least eight entries, and write down what each person wants and fears.

  3. Learn your project's vocabulary: redundancy level, IT load per hall, RFS dates and who owns each long-lead item.

  4. Ask your superintendent and MEP manager which three stakeholders worry them most, and why.

1.12Life-Guide Toolkit for Chapter 1#

In plain English

A data center turns electricity into reliable computing. Your job as a builder is to deliver megawatts of capacity on a date, safely and within budget. The people who control power, money and permits matter more than anyone's title on an organization chart, so find them, understand them, and keep them informed before they have to ask.

Your key responsibilities in this chapter

  • Understand the owner's business case (what a month of delay costs) and keep it in front of the team.

  • Draw and maintain the contract chain and the stakeholder register.

  • Make sure every decision has a named decision-maker (see the RACI below).

  • Set communication channels early: who talks to the utility, the tenant, the neighbors, the authorities.

  • Protect the team from side-door instructions that bypass cost and schedule control.

ANATOMY OF A DATA CENTER: POWER, COOLING, SPACE AND LIFE SAFETY

What is physically inside the building and how do the systems depend on each other?

14 min read · 2,914 words

Big ideaThe big idea#

A data center is two chains and a room. The power chain brings electricity from the utility to every server, with backup in case anything fails. The cooling chain removes the heat those servers produce. The room, the data hall, is where they meet. Nearly every problem you will manage on a data center project is an interface problem: where one system hands off to another. If you can draw these chains from memory and say which trade, which equipment and which test is responsible for each link, you can read any drawing set and any schedule.

The key physical fact: almost every watt of electricity that enters a server leaves as heat. An 8 MW data hall must reject about 8 MW of heat continuously, plus the heat from power conversion losses. That single fact explains why electrical and mechanical systems dominate the cost.

Redundancy in plain language

Imagine the load is a patient on life support. N means one machine; if it fails, the patient is at risk. N+1 means one extra machine ready. 2N means two completely separate sets of machines on two completely separate power paths. Delta Ridge uses N+1 on generators and cooling, and A/B dual power paths (a 2N-style arrangement) to each rack, so that one entire path can be shut down for maintenance without turning off a server. More redundancy means more equipment, more space, more cost and more testing.

How to read an electrical single-line diagram (the most valuable skill you can learn)

  1. Find the source (utility or generator) at the top.

  2. Follow each line downward through breakers, switchgear, transformers, UPS and panels. Each symbol is a piece of equipment someone must buy, deliver, set, connect, test and commission.

  3. Note the voltage at each level; it tells you the cable size, the safety category and the spacing.

  4. Find the tie breakers between systems; they define redundancy.

  5. List the equipment and check each against your procurement log.

2.2The cooling chain, link by link#

Heat is measured in kilowatts or in tons of refrigeration (one ton is about 3.517 kW).

Cooling tons = Heat load in kW ÷ 3.517

For one Delta Ridge hall: 8,000 kW of IT load plus roughly 500 kW of power conversion losses and other heat is about 8,500 kW, or about 2,420 tons. With chillers of 650 tons each, four units carry the load (4 × 650 = 2,600 tons), and a fifth provides N+1 redundancy. That is five chillers per hall and twenty for the building.

Link What it does Notes
Chillers or other heat rejection Remove heat from the water loop to outside air (air-cooled) or to cooling towers (water-cooled) Air-cooled uses less water but more electricity in hot climates; water-cooled is more efficient but uses water and needs treatment
Chilled-water piping and pumps Carry cold water to the halls and warm water back Large-diameter pipe, welding, flushing, pressure testing, and redundant loops
CRAH units, fan walls, coils or liquid cooling Transfer heat from the room air (or directly from the chips) to the water Air cooling works up to a certain density; high-density AI racks often use direct liquid cooling, rear-door heat exchangers or similar
Containment (hot aisle or cold aisle) Prevents hot and cold air from mixing Simple physically but critical for efficiency
Controls Maintain temperature, humidity and flow, and react to failures Software and integration risk
Water treatment and storage Protect pipes; provide ride-through water supply Often overlooked until late

Density is changing the industry. Traditional enterprise racks drew a few kilowatts; modern cloud racks draw more; AI racks can draw many times that, which pushes designs toward liquid cooling, heavier piping, more electrical capacity per square foot, and designs that change during construction. If your project involves high density, expect more design iteration and more change orders, and plan for it in the schedule and the contingency.

2.3The white space: where it all comes together#

The data hall holds rows of racks, overhead cable trays and busways, containment, lighting, fire detection and sometimes a raised floor. Its construction is simple compared with the plant that serves it, but it is where precision and cleanliness count.

  • Floors: flat, level, sealed, designed for heavy rack loads; dust control matters, because construction debris damages equipment.

  • Overhead congestion: cable tray, busway, fiber tray, sprinkler or clean-agent piping, lighting and cooling pipes compete for space. Coordinate in 3D before fabrication.

  • Cleanliness: halls are typically brought to a controlled, clean state before equipment arrives: sealed concrete, sticky mats, restricted access and cleaning protocols.

  • Security: layered access, cameras, mantraps, and sometimes tenant-specific cages.

2.4Fire protection and life safety#

Data centers contain valuable equipment and many ignition sources, so fire protection is both a life-safety and a business-continuity system. Typical elements include very early smoke detection (aspirating detectors that sample the air), pre-action sprinkler or gaseous / clean-agent suppression (chosen by the owner and the authority), special systems for battery rooms, fire-rated walls, smoke control and emergency lighting. Requirements depend on local codes, insurers and owner standards, with standards such as the applicable NFPA codes commonly referenced. The fire marshal and the insurer are real stakeholders, and integrated tests of life-safety systems are among the last and most schedule-sensitive tasks (Chapter 9).

2.5Controls, monitoring and security#

  • BMS (building management system): controls mechanical equipment.

  • EPMS (electrical power monitoring system): monitors the electrical chain.

  • DCIM (data center infrastructure management): the owner's software view of power, cooling and capacity.

  • Security and network systems: access control, video, intrusion detection, structured cabling.

Controls are where integration risk hides. Equipment from many vendors must talk to one another, and software testing happens late. Treat controls integration as a critical path activity, not an afterthought.

2.6The shell: structure and site#

The building is typically a large, low-rise structure of tilt-up concrete, precast panels or steel framing on a heavy slab, with roof loads from mechanical equipment, large equipment pads, generator yards, and fuel storage, plus a site that includes the substation, stormwater controls, security fencing and roads for heavy deliveries. Structure and site are the least risky and most repeatable parts of the job, which makes them good candidates for prefabrication and standardization.

2.9Stakeholder intervention scenarios#

SCENARIO 1The engineer and the equipment manufacturer disagree on the switchgear layout

The manufacturer says the clearance in front of the switchgear is a safety requirement; the engineer says the drawing already meets code. The electrical room cannot fit both.

  1. Get both positions in writing with references to the code section and the product's installation manual.

  2. Hold a joint call with the engineer, manufacturer, electrical sub and your MEP manager; use a mark-up of the room layout.

  3. Choose the more conservative requirement when safety is involved, and use the RFI process to get the engineer to confirm in writing.

  4. Assess impact: can the room be enlarged, or can equipment be reoriented? Price the options.

  5. Record the decision and issue revised drawings.

SCENARIO 2The owner's standard conflicts with the local fire marshal

The owner's global standard specifies one suppression design; the local fire marshal asks for another.

  1. Meet the fire marshal early, with the owner's fire protection engineer.

  2. Ask what outcome they need (life safety, containment, evacuation) rather than arguing about the system.

  3. Present the owner's performance data and the insurer's requirements.

  4. Seek a documented agreement and obtain approval before ordering equipment.

SCENARIO 3The tenant requests liquid cooling in Hall 3 after the design is complete

  1. Treat it as a design change. Request the revised design basis, power and cooling loads.

  2. Quantify impact on piping, structure, electrical capacity, schedule and cost; present options and decision dates.

  3. Protect procurement: identify items already ordered that might be affected and the cost to modify or cancel.

  4. Negotiate a change order with time, not just cost.

2.11A field story (composite)#

Two weeks before the first generator arrives, a project engineer compares the delivery drawing with the pad design and sees that the pad is 8 inches shorter than the generator's base. The concrete sub had followed an earlier revision. It costs the project about $6,000 and two days to extend the pad; had it been found after the generator was set, the cost would have been far higher. The lesson: compare the real equipment drawing to the real installation drawing, every time.

  • Believing that "redundancy" is just drawing extra boxes: it must be tested.

  • Neglecting controls and integration.

  • Forgetting that cooling equipment is also an electrical load.

  • Allowing construction dust into clean spaces.

PRECONSTRUCTION: SITE, POWER, PERMITS, DESIGN AND THE ESTIMATE

How do site, power, permits, design and the estimate come together before construction?

16 min read · 3,266 words

Big ideaThe big idea#

Most of a data center project's cost, schedule and risk are decided before the first shovel of dirt moves. The site determines the power. The power determines the schedule. The design determines the long-lead equipment. The estimate and the procurement strategy determine the money. A project manager who joins at preconstruction and does these well makes the construction phase far easier; a PM who inherits a weak preconstruction spends two years fixing it.

On Delta Ridge the early work happened in an early works agreement beginning in September 2026, ahead of the main notice to proceed on January 4, 2027. Think of preconstruction as five parallel streams: site, power, permits, design and money.

3.1Site selection and due diligence#

By the time a contractor is hired, the owner has usually chosen the site, but the PM still needs to understand why, because every site factor becomes a project risk.

Power availability How many MW can the utility deliver, by when, at what cost? The first and often the decisive question; sets the phasing and the critical path
Fiber and connectivity Are multiple diverse fiber routes available? Customers will not lease without it; may need trenching on public rights-of-way

Zoning and community Is a data center permitted? How will neighbors react? Entitlement time and risk

Due diligence for the PM: read the geotechnical report and its limitations, the environmental assessment, the survey, the utility study, the zoning approvals and any development agreement. Each document contains commitments you will be asked to honor: noise limits, hours of work, truck routes, buffers, landscaping, and reporting duties.

3.2Utility power: the longest road in the project#

Utility power is both the most valuable and the least controllable part of a data center project. The process normally follows these steps, and each one takes months:

Step What happens Typical owner and utility responsibilities
1. Load request and capacity study The utility studies whether its grid can supply the requested MW Owner submits load profile and ramp schedule
2. Service / interconnection agreement A contract defining capacity, schedule, costs and obligations Owner signs; cost sharing and deposits are negotiated
3. Facilities study and design Utility designs the new lines and substation connection Owner designs the on-site substation if applicable
4. Long-lead procurement Large power transformers, breakers, relays, conductors Whoever buys them must order very early
5. Construction Utility builds its side; owner or GC builds its side Coordination of outages, access, inspection
6. Testing and energization Protection systems tested; utility approves; power is switched on A formal, scheduled event with its own approvals
7. Permanent power and ramp-up Load is added in steps agreed with the utility The owner must not exceed approved capacity

On Delta Ridge, the owner reserved and ordered the main 138 kV to 13.8 kV transformers in mid-2025, before the GC was hired, because market lead times for large power transformers have recently run from roughly a year to well over two years (verify current figures with suppliers). The utility's target for permanent power energization is November 15, 2027, which sets the date for large-scale commissioning. That date is on the critical path of the whole project.

Power strategies that protect the schedule

  • Temporary power for construction: sized and permitted early, since it is needed for tools, lighting, welding and eventually early testing.

  • Phased capacity: if the utility can deliver only part of the power initially (for example 24 MW out of 32 MW), the owner may commission Halls 1 to 3 first and Hall 4 later.

  • Bridging with generators: temporary generation or the permanent generators can support some testing before utility power arrives, subject to permits and fuel logistics.

  • Owner-led utility coordination: the owner's team usually leads, but the PM must track every utility milestone as if it were their own.

The PM's procedure for permits:

  1. Build the tracker in week one with owner, authority, submission date, needed-by date, status and risk.

  2. Identify items with long review cycles and start those first (air permits, wetlands, utilities).

  3. Meet each reviewer face to face when possible; keep a log of every comment and response.

  4. Release work in permit phases so site work and foundations can start while later permits are pending.

  5. Never start work that a pending permit covers. The owner and you carry the legal and financial risk.

Electrical and mechanical systems (including fire protection and controls) total about $252 million, or roughly 63 percent of the price. The shell and site total only $70 million, or 17.5 percent. That is why a data center PM must be fluent in electrical and mechanical scope.

Markup versus margin

The fee is $16.0 million on a cost of $384.0 million. That is a markup on cost of 4.17 percent, but a margin on price of 4.0 percent.

Price = Cost ÷ (1 - Margin) = 384.0 ÷ 0.96 = 400.0

Adding 4 percent to cost would price the job at $399.4 million, giving away about $0.6 million. Always know whether your company, your contract and your change order markups work on cost or on price.

Escalation, allowances and exclusions

  • Escalation: prices for equipment and labor rise over time. Identify when quotes expire, include an escalation allowance where purchases occur far in the future, and lock in prices through early orders.

  • Allowances: placeholders for work not yet defined (for example, a security system allowance until the owner decides). Track each allowance and who decides.

  • Exclusions: costs not in the GMP, such as owner-furnished IT equipment, utility charges, land and the owner's own staff. Know the list.

3.7The estimate-to-project handover#

Before buyout, the PM and the estimator spend half a day reviewing: assumptions and exclusions; allowances and contingency; quotes relied on; productivity and duration assumptions; risks the estimator worries about; clarifications sent to the owner; and the GMP's treatment of design growth. Write minutes; they become your first risk register.

3.8Stakeholder intervention scenarios#

SCENARIO 1The owner wants a firm GMP now, but design is only 40 percent complete

  1. Explain the difference between a price and a prediction: at 40 percent, big assumptions remain.

  2. Offer a path: an early works package now, a GMP at 60 percent with a defined assumptions list, and a transparent design growth allowance.

  3. Show examples of change order exposure at low design completion, using your own history.

  4. Agree a decision calendar with dates for design milestones and the GMP.

  5. Document the owner's choice, including any pressure to set the GMP early.

Influence principle: people accept a delay in commitment when they see the cost of premature commitment.

SCENARIO 2The utility's study says only 24 MW will be available at first

  1. Verify the conditions and the date of the upgrade that would allow 32 MW.

  2. Develop a phased power plan with the owner: Halls 1 to 3 energized first, Hall 4 later, with temporary generation or demand limits as options.

  3. Reprice and reschedule the phasing; protect procurement for Hall 4 equipment (delay orders where possible, or reserve slots).

  4. Tell the tenant early and give them options.

SCENARIO 3The air regulator says the generator model must change

  1. Find out exactly what drives the requirement: emissions limits, hours of operation, or distance to receptors.

  2. Meet the regulator with the manufacturer's emissions data and the owner's environmental consultant.

  3. Compare options: a lower-emission engine model, catalytic treatment systems, reduced testing hours, or different permit classification.

  4. Assess the equipment order: has it been placed? What are cancellation and change costs?

  5. Decide with the owner and update the schedule.

  • Ignoring the utility's schedule until it slips.

  • Releasing construction before permits and approvals are in hand.

  • Letting the GMP exclude important design growth without an agreed process.

  • Forgetting that long-lead equipment decisions must be made long before drawings are complete.

CONTRACTS, PROCUREMENT AND THE LONG-LEAD SUPPLY CHAIN

How are contracts and long-lead equipment bought and protected?

17 min read · 3,557 words

Big ideaThe big idea#

On a data center, procurement is construction. The most expensive and most schedule-critical items are not built on site; they are manufactured in factories months or years earlier. A contract decides who carries the risk if those items are late or cost more. A PM who understands the contract, buys the right items at the right time, and tracks every factory milestone protects the schedule, the budget and the company's profit.

4.1The contract: a risk-allocation document#

A contract is not just a legal document; it answers one question over and over: if something goes wrong, who pays, who fixes it, and how fast must they act? You do not need to be a lawyer, but you must know where the clauses are and what deadlines they impose. Always read the full contract set in your first week, and build a one-page cheat sheet.

The contract documents and order of precedence

A construction contract is a bundle: the agreement (parties, price, time, payment), the general conditions, supplementary conditions for the project, specifications, drawings, addenda and modifications, and on a CMAR project the GMP exhibit with its assumptions, exclusions and allowances. An order of precedence clause says which document wins in a conflict. Check it.

4.5The procurement lifecycle and the log that tracks it#

  1. Specification and RFQ: clear technical requirements, delivery milestones, testing, warranty, shipping terms.

  2. Bid leveling: compare scope, not price (see below).

  3. Award and PO or subcontract: include delivery dates, liquidated damages for late delivery where possible, payment milestones tied to progress, and rights to inspect the factory.

  4. Submittals: shop drawings, data sheets, test plans; reviewed by the engineer and the owner.

  5. Release for manufacturing: after approval.

  6. Manufacturing progress: monthly or biweekly factory reports; factory visits for critical items.

  7. Factory acceptance test (FAT): witnessed testing before shipment; punch list closed before release.

  8. Shipping: route survey, permits, escorts, insurance, title transfer.

  9. Receiving: inspect for damage, check against the approved submittal, photograph, record serial numbers.

  10. Storage: protect from weather and humidity; maintain manufacturer's storage requirements (batteries and switchgear are sensitive).

  11. Setting and installation: rigging, pad checks, alignment.

  12. Start-up and commissioning (Chapter 9).

The procurement log: one row per item with spec section, supplier, PO value, order date, submittal status, factory milestones, ship date, need-on-site date, float in weeks, risk rating, owner of follow-up. Review it weekly. Float in weeks is the number that matters: items with under four weeks of float get daily attention.

4.6Buying out the trades#

In addition to equipment, the GC buys the labor and installation scopes from subcontractors. On a data center, the electrical and mechanical subcontractors are your most important partners, and skilled labor availability is often the real constraint.

Prequalification

Ask for: financial statements and bonding capacity, safety record, experience on comparable mission-critical projects, the names and track record of the proposed foremen and superintendents, current workload, and a manpower plan showing how many electricians or pipefitters they will provide month by month. A low price from a firm that cannot staff the job is not a bargain.

4.9Stakeholder intervention scenarios#

SCENARIO 1The generator manufacturer announces a 10-week slip on the first five units

The units serve Hall 1, on the critical path. Ten weeks is 50 working days; at general conditions of roughly $55,000 per working day that is about $2.75 million, and if the delay is yours, LDs of $40,000 per calendar day for 70 days add $2.8 million.

  1. Verify the cause and the recovery plan in writing from the manufacturer's project executive.

  2. Look for slot swaps: ask the manufacturer to ship the five units originally scheduled for Hall 4 (which has float) to Hall 1 and move the delay to Hall 4. This can cost almost nothing.

  3. Explore partial shipments, additional factory shifts or alternate units.

  4. Consider temporary generators for commissioning activities that need backup power, subject to permits.

  5. Notify the owner with the recovery options and costs; if the delay is caused by a supplier you control, the exposure is yours; if an owner-furnished item, notify under that clause.

  6. Update the schedule with the new delivery and show the effect on Hall 1 and Hall 4.

Influence principle: suppliers respond to a clear, data-based request that makes it easy for them to say yes: offer them a solution rather than only a complaint.

SCENARIO 2Owner-furnished switchgear arrives six weeks late; the owner blames the GC

  1. Check the contract for who owns delivery risk and what notice was required.

  2. Produce the record: emails, procurement log entries and meeting minutes showing the delivery dates and your notices.

  3. Meet the owner's representative and show a one-page delay analysis; propose a recovery plan and ask who funds it.

  4. Agree a path: time extension and cost under the contract's owner-delay provisions, or an agreed acceleration plan.

SCENARIO 3The electrical sub says it cannot staff the project as promised

  1. Meet the principal, not just the foreman. Understand the real constraint.

  2. Compare the manpower plan with the actual numbers and the schedule requirement.

  3. Agree a recovery plan in writing with dates and numbers; invoke the subcontract's manpower clause if needed.

  4. Identify a second electrical contractor to cover a section (for example, cable pulling for Halls 3 and 4).

  5. Consider incentives or prefabrication to reduce field labor.

  6. Look at your side: access, drawings, material, and payment.

SCENARIO 4A tariff-driven 6 percent increase on $38 million of generators

  1. Read the contract: does it allow price adjustment for tariffs or changes in law? Deadlines for notice?

  2. Quantify: 6 percent of $38.0 million is $2.28 million.

  3. Document the notice from the supplier and the date; send formal notice to the owner within the contract limit.

  4. Negotiate with the supplier: price validity, volume, alternative sources, partial relief.

  5. Seek owner agreement on sharing or change order if the clause permits; otherwise assess the impact on contingency.

PLANNING AND SCHEDULING FOR SPEED TO MARKET

How is the schedule built, protected and recovered?

14 min read · 3,016 words

Big ideaThe big idea#

On a data center, the schedule is the revenue. A hall that opens a month late can cost the owner a month of rent from a tenant who is already waiting, plus penalties and lost reputation. A good schedule is not a pretty bar chart; it is a logic model of the project that answers three questions: what must not slip, what can slip and by how much, and what will it cost us if it does.

5.1The building blocks, applied to a data center#

  • Activity: a task with a duration (for example, "Set generators on pads, Hall 1").

  • Logic links: which activities must finish before the next can start. Finish-to-start is most common; start-to-start and finish-to-finish links, with lags, capture overlap and curing or waiting time.

  • Milestone: a zero-duration event, such as "Permanent power energized" or "Hall 1 ready for service."

  • Work breakdown structure (WBS): organizes activities by area (site, shell, Hall 1, Hall 2, etc.) and by system (power, cooling, fire, controls).

  • Critical path: the longest chain of dependent activities from start to finish; it sets the minimum duration. Float is the amount an activity can slip without delaying the finish.

Total float = Late start - Early start

Activities with zero float are critical. Non-critical activities share their float; once it is used, they become critical.

What is different on a data center: logic must include items that office buildings rarely model: utility milestones, equipment factory tests and shipping, owner and tenant reviews, energization approvals, commissioning levels, load bank availability, and hall-by-hall turnover. A schedule that omits these is a drawing, not a plan.

5.2The master schedule, drawn#

The chart shows Delta Ridge from a notice to proceed on January 4, 2027 through completion of Hall 4 in August 2028. Read it from left to right and look for three things.

  1. Two near-critical chains. The utility chain (permits, substation and feed, permanent power on November 15, 2027) and the equipment chain (factory production, delivery, setting, electrical installation) converge on Hall 1's commissioning. The shell chain (site, foundations, roof and envelope) must reach "dry-in" before equipment can be set indoors. A delay in any of them moves Hall 1.

  2. Overlap. Electrical and cooling installation for Hall 1 start before the shell is fully closed in the last bays. Overlap saves time and creates congestion and risk.

  3. Staggered halls. Halls 2 to 4 are fitted out and commissioned while Hall 1 is already turned over. Each live hall changes the safety rules for the entire site (Chapter 8).

5.6Recovery and acceleration: the toolbox and the math#

When the schedule slips, a good PM analyzes before spending.

  1. Verify the update. Are actual dates right? Is the logic realistic?

  2. Find the real drivers on the critical path and assign responsibility (owner, designer, contractor, utility, neutral).

  3. Send notices for delays that are not yours.

  4. List options from cheapest to costliest: resequence and overlap; add crews in separate zones; extend working hours; weekend or second shift work; prefabrication and modularization; bring in more equipment; expedite deliveries; reduce scope with owner approval.

  5. Price each option and the days it recovers.

  6. Compare the cost per working day recovered with the value of a day.

Worked example. Hall 1 is 15 working days late. The value of a day if the delay is yours is about $55,000 + (1.4 × $40,000) = $111,000 per working day.

Option Cost ($) Days recovered Cost per day recovered ($) Verdict
A: Second shift for Hall 1 cable pulling 1,400,000 15 93,333 Worth it: below $111,000
B: Saturday overtime for all MEP trades 2,200,000 15 146,667 Not worth it unless tenant penalties or strategic value justify
C: Prefabricated cable assemblies 900,000 8 112,500 About break-even

Be cautious about acceleration through long overtime: sustained overtime reduces productivity and raises safety and quality risk. Studies and experience suggest diminishing returns when crews work extended weeks for many weeks; plan changes of shift or crew rather than relying on overtime alone.

5.7Site logistics for a data center campus#

  • Peak workforce: a campus like Delta Ridge may reach 1,000 or more workers at peak. Parking, shuttles, restrooms, break areas and badging must be planned.

  • Heavy lifts and haul routes: cranes, generator and transformer deliveries, route surveys, permits, escorts, and ground bearing capacity.

  • Laydown and staging: equipment arrives in large pieces; storage needs protection and security.

  • Temporary power and utilities: need early design and permits; temporary generators and fuel storage.

  • Separation of live and under-construction areas: once Hall 1 is energized and commissioned, access control, fire barriers, dust control, vibration limits, and lockout/tagout rules are enforced across the project.

  • Security: badging, background checks, tool and vehicle inspections.

5.8Updating the schedule: a monthly and weekly rhythm#

  1. Baseline: approved original plan.

  2. Weekly: update the lookahead, PPC, procurement float.

  3. Monthly: record actual dates and remaining durations, recalculate, compare to baseline, and review the critical path.

  4. Document reasons for every delay by cause and responsibility (Chapter 10 covers claims).

  5. Publish a one-page schedule summary with top five risks and decisions needed.

COST MANAGEMENT: BUDGET, FORECAST, BILLING, CASH FLOW AND CHANGE ORDERS

How are cost, billing, cash and change orders controlled?

17 min read · 3,585 words

Big ideaThe big idea#

On a $400 million project, a 1 percent mistake is $4 million. Cost management is not recording what you spent; it is forecasting what the project will cost when it is finished, early enough to act, billing accurately so cash flows, and pricing changes so that scope growth is paid for. The best PMs can state, at any moment, with evidence: we will finish at this cost, this profit, this cash position, and here is why it moved.

6.1From estimate to budget#

The estimate from Chapter 3 becomes the budget, loaded into the cost system by cost code: each trade package, each major equipment item, each general conditions line, and contingency in its own code. Keep general conditions broken down (staff, trailers, hoisting, temporary power, cleaning), because they grow with time.

Rule: one cost code, one owner, one budget. If two people can charge to a code, nobody controls it.

After buyout (Chapter 4), each trade budget is replaced by the actual subcontract or purchase order. Any difference is a buyout variance: Delta Ridge's was a $6.0 million loss against a $340.0 million direct budget, drawn from the $14.0 million contingency.

The contingency log

Contingency is a shared risk reserve, controlled by a named person, released by written memo with cause and cost code, and compared each month with the risks that remain.

Month Draw ($M) Reason Balance ($M)
Opening 14.0

Month 4 1.4 Rock at the substation not in the geotechnical report (partially offset by change order) 6.6

At month 10, $3.8 million remains while commissioning, utility and tenant-change risks may exceed that. The honest message to the owner and the project executive is: the contingency is thinner than the remaining risk. Raise it now, not at month 18.

6.2Forecasting: EAC by cost code#

For every cost code:

EAC = Cost to date + Estimated cost to complete

EAC (estimate at completion) is not "budget minus spent." The cost to complete is your best estimate of the remaining work using real commitments, productivity trends and risks. Using budget minus spent hides overruns until it is too late.

Worked example: Electrical installation (month 10)

Item Amount ($M)

Pending change requests the sub has submitted (risk-weighted: $1.8M at 50 percent likelihood) 0.9
Forecast final cost (EAC) 85.6
Variance vs. original budget −5.6

The forecast includes pending exposure, because waiting until a claim is approved to forecast it is how surprises are born. It also includes the productivity trend: if cable pulling output is 15 percent below plan, the forecast needs more labor hours, whether or not the sub has yet asked for money.

On a data center it often comes from billing stored equipment ahead of installation. Under-billing means you are financing the owner, often because of late or incomplete pay applications. An ethical rule: the forecast must be honest. Do not leave a known overrun out of EAC to protect a month's report. Hidden losses that surface late have ended careers and companies.

The monthly forecast procedure

  1. Close the month and reconcile commitments with the subcontract and PO logs.

  2. Update each code's EAC with the superintendent and MEP manager: remaining quantities, productivity, manpower.

  3. Add pending and potential changes with probability, on both cost and revenue.

  4. Review general conditions: months remaining × monthly burn; if the schedule extends, quantify it.

  5. Review contingency against remaining risks.

  6. Bridge profit from last month and from the original.

  7. Agree actions with the project executive.

  8. Write a short narrative: numbers say what; narrative says why and what next.

  5. Assemble the GC application and internal review.

  6. Submit on time; a day late can cost a month.

  7. Follow up, answer comments quickly, and track the payment date.

  8. Pay subs according to contract and law.

6.5Cash flow: the deposit problem#

Profit on paper is not cash in the bank. On data centers the biggest cash risk is deposits on long-lead equipment paid at order, long before the owner pays for installed work.

Lien waivers: collect conditional waivers with the application and unconditional waivers only after payment clears, from every tier entitled to lien rights. Rules vary by state.

6.6Change management#

Change is normal on a data center, especially with tenant requirements and rising density. The problem is not change; it is undocumented, unpriced, unapproved change. Each change answers three questions: what changed, what does it cost, and what does it do to the schedule?

Why changes happen

  • Owner or tenant-driven: capacity increases, new cooling approaches, extra metering, security requirements.

  • Design-driven: conflicts, omissions, code comments.

  • Field-driven: differing site conditions, equipment dimensions that differ from drawings.

  • External: utility requirements, regulator conditions, supply disruptions.

The change process

  1. Identify and give written notice within the contract's time limit.

  2. Document facts: source, date, drawings, photos.

  3. Define scope precisely and mark drawings.

  4. Get sub pricing with labor hours, material quantities, equipment, markup and time.

  5. Check the numbers against benchmarks, scope in the base contract and markup limits.

  6. Add your markup exactly as the contract specifies.

  7. Assess time impact using schedule logic and request time where the critical path is affected.

  8. Submit the proposal; negotiate with facts.

  9. Execute the change order; update subcontracts, budget, schedule and the next pay application.

  10. Log every step.

If you must proceed before price is agreed (a change directive), track cost on T&M tickets signed daily by the owner's representative; unsigned tickets are the classic reason work goes unpaid.

6.8Stakeholder intervention scenarios#

  2. Ask the owner's representative to channel tenant requests through a single weekly change meeting with a log.

  3. Offer a design freeze calendar with dates after which changes carry added schedule consequences.

  4. Price a standing menu of likely changes (extra metering, added circuits) so decisions are faster.

  5. Document every informal request with a same-day email.

SCENARIO 2A sub claims 70 percent complete when the field shows 50 percent

  1. Walk the work together with quantities and photographs; agree line by line.

  2. If the sub is short of cash, solve with legitimate tools (approved stored materials, a deposit line) instead of overpaying.

  3. Hold the line on retainage and lien waivers.

  4. Explain why: if the sub fails later, you pay the cost to complete plus the money already overpaid.

SCENARIO 3The owner takes eight weeks to approve a $2.65 million change

  1. Remind the owner of pricing validity and the schedule consequence.

  2. Ask: "What do you need from us to approve this by Friday?"

  3. Add it to every weekly agenda with a named owner and date.

  4. If work must continue, request a written directive and track T&M.

  5. Carry the cost as exposure in your forecast regardless of approval date.

  1. Explain the risk of hiding losses and the effect on lenders and sureties.

  2. Show the numbers and propose to forecast it with a probability and a recovery plan.

  3. Offer a compromise: a transparent range with assumptions.

  4. Escalate to compliance or the executive team if the pressure continues. Never alter records.

How does the building actually get built, phase by phase?

Big ideaThe big idea#

A data center goes up in a sequence driven by physics and access: you cannot set a generator before its pad is poured, pull cable before the conduit is in, test a chiller before power is on, or hand a hall to a tenant before it is clean. Learn the sequence and you can predict what will be needed, where, and by whom, weeks ahead. 7.3Phase B: Shell and envelope (months 5 to 10)#

What happens. Walls rise (often tilt-up concrete or precast panels), steel or concrete roof structure goes on, the roof is waterproofed and fitted for heavy rooftop and wall-mounted equipment, and the envelope is closed in.   • Roof design must carry equipment loads, penetrations and walkways without leaks; plan the roofing and mechanical curbs together.

  • Crane picks for panels and heavy roof loads need detailed lift plans, ground bearing checks and weather limits.

7.4Phase C: Major equipment: receive, protect, rig and set (months 8 to 12)#

What happens. Generators, transformers, switchgear, UPS modules, battery cabinets, PDUs, chillers and CRAH units arrive, often one truck at a time over months, and are placed on pads or in rooms. This is the most visible, most dangerous and most schedule-sensitive physical step on the project.

The receiving and setting procedure.

  1. Delivery plan: time, route, escorts, unloading area, crane or hydraulic jack availability.

  2. Receiving inspection: check for shipping damage (tilt indicators, impact recorders, scratches), compare to the approved submittal and PO, photograph, record serial numbers, note damage on the delivery ticket before signing.

  3. Engineered lift plan: equipment weight, crane capacity chart, ground bearing, swing radius, weather limits, signal person, exclusion zone.

  4. Pad verification: dimensions, elevations, anchor locations, embeds, level.

  5. Set and anchor: alignment, shimming, grouting as specified.

  6. Protection: covers, desiccant, space heaters (some equipment must stay warm and dry), no cutting or welding nearby without protection.

  7. Document: as-set photographs, anchor torque records.

7.5Phase D: Electrical distribution and cable (months 9 to 14)#

What happens. This phase makes the power chain real. Medium-voltage cable is pulled through duct banks and terminated; low-voltage feeders connect switchgear, UPS and PDUs; overhead busway runs above the racks; grounding and bonding are completed; everything is labeled, torque-checked and tested.

  • Terminations of medium-voltage cable are specialized work: qualified splicers, strict procedures and testing.

  • Prefabricated assemblies (for example, factory-made cable sets and busway sections) save field hours and improve quality.

  • Electrical acceptance testing (insulation resistance, torque checks, protective relay tests and similar) follows recognized standards and is documented before energization.

  • Labeling is not decoration: operators must be able to identify any circuit in an emergency.

Tracking productivity (a worked example)

Suppose Hall 1 requires about 420,000 feet of feeder and branch cable pulled, at a planned production of 3,200 feet per crew-day.

Crew-days = Quantity ÷ Production per crew-day

  • At plan: 420,000 ÷ 3,200 = 131 crew-days.

  • At actual 2,700 feet per crew-day (84 percent of plan): 420,000 ÷ 2,700 = 156 crew-days, 25 more.

With four crews, that is about six extra working days. Meanwhile, if a crew-day costs $5,700 (labor, tools, supervision), the extra cost is about $142,500 for Hall 1. The PM's response is to find the cause: access, congestion, material not staged, drawings, crew mix, rework? Then fix it, add a crew or an additional shift, or prefabricate for Halls 2 to 4. 7.8Phase G: White space, support areas and the repeating halls#

What happens. Hall floors are sealed, cleaned and protected; containment and overhead systems are completed; support areas (offices, loading dock, security) are finished; and the hall reaches a "ready for tenant" cleanliness and access-control standard.

Repetition and the learning curve

Because four halls are essentially identical, the second, third and fourth should be faster and cheaper than the first if the team captures lessons. A typical illustrative pattern of field labor hours per hall:

Working beside a live hall

Once Hall 1 is energized and turned over, the rest of the site is a construction site next to a live data center. Rules multiply: controlled access, dust and vibration limits, no work on live equipment without a written permit, clear separation of construction and operating power, and coordination with the owner's operations team on every shutdown or switching event.

7.9Managing labor, productivity and the workforce#

  • Manpower curve: Delta Ridge may peak around 1,100 workers in months 12 to 14. The MEP trades dominate the peak. Plan parking, shuttles, restrooms, break areas, badging and orientation capacity.

  • Skilled labor shortage: electricians, pipefitters and commissioning technicians are scarce. Secure commitments early, use prefabrication, protect productivity (clear access, material at point of use), and treat retention and travel logistics as project risks.

  • Union and open-shop rules: know jurisdictional and work-rule differences on your project.

  • Daily productivity routine: morning huddle, material staged, work packages ready, end-of-day progress recorded.

  • Safety and fatigue: long hours reduce alertness; plan shifts and breaks.

SCENARIO 1A near miss during a generator lift

A tag line catches a worker's sleeve; no one is injured, but the lift was proceeding with a worker inside the exclusion zone.

  1. Stop the lift and secure the load. Check on the worker.

  2. Hold a short, calm discussion with the lift director, rigger and crew: what happened, what the plan required.

  3. Reinforce the rule (no one in the exclusion zone), adjust the plan if the layout invited the error, and re-brief everyone before work resumes.

  4. Report per company and legal requirements; share lessons with all trades the next morning.

  5. Recognize the worker who reported it.

Influence principle: if the first response to a near miss is thanks and action, people keep reporting.

SCENARIO 2The tenant's technical representative rejects the busway installation as not meeting their standard

  1. Ask for specifics: which requirement, which location, which spec section.

  2. Compare with the approved submittal, the mock-up and the contract documents. If the work conforms, show it; if the tenant's standard is stricter, it is a change.

  3. Offer a joint walk with the engineer and the owner's representative.

  4. Agree a path: correct, accept as is, or price a change. Record the decision.

  5. Prevent recurrence with an approved mock-up and sign-off for each repeat hall.

SCENARIO 3The utility offers only one weekend for a planned outage and the work needs two

  1. Verify the true duration with the electrical sub; review each step.

  2. Prepare minute-by-minute switching and work plans; rehearse; stage all materials and spares.

  3. Negotiate with the utility: an additional day, parallel crews, or a second outage window.

  4. Prepare a contingency if the energization slips; notify the owner and tenant early.

SCENARIO 4Two crafts dispute who installs a piece of equipment

  1. Get the facts: contract scope, subcontract language, manufacturer instructions, local work rules.

  2. Meet both foremen and their project managers; keep the superintendent present.

  3. Decide by contract and agreed rules, not by who is louder; if unclear, you may have a scope gap you must fill.

  4. Document the decision and adjust the lookahead.

Chapter 8

QUALITY, SAFETY, COORDINATION AND DOCUMENTATION

How do quality, safety, coordination and documents hold it together?

16 min read · 3,279 words

Big ideaThe big idea#

Four disciplines keep a data center project from falling apart: quality (the work is right), safety (everyone goes home), coordination (the systems fit together), and documentation (you can prove all of it). They are one system. A missed inspection becomes a rework, a rework becomes a delay, a delay becomes pressure, and pressure is how people get hurt. The PM who builds all four into the daily routine rarely gets surprised.

8.1Quality: build it right once#

Data centers promise uptime measured in years. A loose lug on a breaker, a weld flaw in a pipe or a missed fire-stop can turn into an outage long after the contractor has left. Quality is cheapest when planned before the work starts and most expensive when found after it is covered. Rework often costs several times the original work, in money, schedule and reputation.

Quality assurance and quality control

  • Quality assurance (QA): the system: plans, procedures, training, who checks what and when.

  • Quality control (QC): the checks themselves: inspections and tests of the actual work.

The three-phase control model

For each major activity (an electrical room, a cable pulling operation, a piping section):

  1. Preparatory phase: before work starts, review the specifications, submittals, materials, methods, inspection requirements and safety hazards with the crew.

  2. Initial phase: when the first section is installed, inspect it against the mock-up or requirements and fix problems before repeating them at scale.

  3. Follow-up phase: continuing checks, with deficiencies documented and corrected.

The inspection and test plan (ITP)

For every system, build a table listing what is checked, by whom, when, to what standard, and what record is produced. A sample for the electrical and mechanical systems of one hall:

Concrete pads and duct banks Rebar, embeds and sleeves before pour; compressive strength tests; location survey Inspection reports, test results, survey
Equipment setting Pad level and dimensions; anchor torque; alignment; protection As-set photos, torque logs
MV and LV cable and terminations Cable type and size; bend radius; termination qualifications; insulation resistance; torque Test sheets, termination records
Busway and PDUs Alignment; support spacing; connections tightened; labeling Inspection checklists
Grounding and bonding Continuity; resistance testing Test reports
Chilled-water piping Weld inspection; supports; flushing; pressure test; insulation Weld logs, test charts, flush reports
Containment and white space Seals; gaps; cleanliness Photo records, cleanliness checks
Fire protection Hydrostatic or air tests; detection device checks; fire-stopping Inspection and acceptance records
Controls Point-to-point verification; calibration Point lists with signatures

Factory witness tests and mock-ups

  • A factory acceptance test (FAT) for generators, switchgear and UPS lets the team find defects at the factory, where they are cheapest to fix. Send a qualified witness with a written test procedure.

  • A mock-up of a typical electrical room corner, a busway tap-off, a pipe connection to a CRAH unit, or a cable tray junction sets the standard. The owner and tenant approve it, and every later installation is measured against it.

Nonconformance: the five-step response

  1. Record it: photo, location, specification reference.

  2. Stop related work if the issue could repeat.

  3. Find the root cause: wrong drawing? unclear detail? poor training? sequence?

  4. Correct it, with design approval if the fix departs from the documents.

  5. Verify and close, then fix the process.

8.2Safety: nobody gets hurt#

Construction is hazardous, and data centers add concentrated electrical energy, heavy equipment and live-site operations. Safety is not a department; it is a condition of every task, planned before work, owned by everyone, and never traded for schedule. Every person on site has the right and duty to stop unsafe work.

Requirements differ by country. In the United States, OSHA's construction standards (29 CFR 1926) set minimum rules, NFPA 70E addresses electrical safety in the workplace, and owners frequently require more. Know the rules that apply where you work and your company's safety manual.

  • Site-specific safety plan covering hazards, responsibilities and emergency response.

  • Activity hazard analysis (AHA) for each task: steps, hazards and controls before work begins.

  • Daily pre-task planning with the crew; weekly toolbox talks.

  • Orientation for every worker and visitor; inspections and audits with findings closed quickly.

  • Permits to work for hot work, confined space, energized work, lifts and shutdowns.

  • Incident and near-miss reporting: near misses are free lessons.

  • Leading indicators (inspections done, hazards corrected, near misses reported, pre-task plans completed) alongside lagging indicators (injury rates).

The energization procedure: a controlled event, not a switch

Energizing a switchgear lineup or a transformer is a planned event. A typical sequence (always follow the owner's, utility's and your company's procedures):

  1. Verify that all installation and acceptance test records are complete and approved.

  2. Confirm the system boundaries, lockout status, and that no one is working inside the equipment.

  3. Hold a pre-energization meeting with everyone affected: electrical sub, commissioning agent, owner's operations, utility, safety.

  4. Walk down the equipment and the area; remove tools and materials; post barriers.

  5. Obtain written authorization from the people named in the procedure.

  6. Energize by qualified personnel following a written switching order, in the planned sequence.

  7. Observe and record readings, then keep the area controlled until the system is declared stable.

Safety culture

Culture is what people do when no one is watching, and what happens when someone raises a concern. If the response is thanks and action, people speak up; if it is annoyance, they stop. The PM and superintendent set that tone every day.

8.3Coordination: making the systems fit#

On a data center, a hall's ceiling space holds cable tray, busway, sprinkler or clean-agent piping, cooling pipes, lighting, detection and containment. Congestion is normal; surprises are not.

BIM and virtual design and construction (VDC)

In a coordination model, each trade models its systems; a coordinator overlays them, finds clashes, and the trades resolve them in meetings before fabrication. Typical outputs: coordinated drawings, sleeve and penetration locations, prefabrication packages, and 4D (time) sequencing. Agree early on the level of detail, who owns each element, and when the model is "frozen" for fabrication.

The ceiling-space priority rule

Agree and publish a rule at the start so that conflicts are resolved by a standard, not by personality. A common order is: gravity drains first (they need slope), then large pipes and ducts, then sprinkler or suppression mains, then cable tray and busway, then small items. Adjust to your project's logic and the engineer's approval.

Field verification

Models are only as good as the field. Verify as-built conditions (structure, embeds, installed equipment) before fabricating; survey critical points; walk the work with foremen.

SCENARIO 1An owner executive pressures you to keep working through a failed safety inspection to protect the date

  1. Do not argue; offer facts. Describe the specific hazard, the regulation, and the outcome if the work continues.

  2. Offer a way to protect the date safely: fix the hazard in a few hours; shift crews to other areas; add a crew tomorrow.

  3. Hold your position on safety and involve your project executive and the owner's safety lead.

  4. Document the conversation and decision.

Influence principle: show how the safe path serves the owner's goals (the injury, stop-work order or insurance consequences are worse than a day's delay).

SCENARIO 2A sub's foreman bypasses lockout because "it will only take a minute"

  1. Stop the work and ask the worker to step away. Verify the equipment is safe.

  2. Talk calmly and privately with the foreman: ask why, listen, explain the rule and the consequences.

  3. Make compliance easy: locks, tags and a clear authority to ask for help; set a standard that no one is blamed for stopping work.

  4. Follow up with the sub's project manager and, if the pattern persists, apply the consequences in the subcontract.

SCENARIO 3The tenant's inspector fails a busway installation that the engineer already approved

  1. Ask for the specific requirement and which document sets it.

  2. Compare: is it in the contract documents, the tenant's standard, or a new interpretation?

  3. Meet the tenant's inspector, the engineer and the owner's representative in the field.

  4. Agree a resolution: correct, accept as is with a documented decision, or price a change.

  5. Record the standard for the repeat halls.

SCENARIO 4Design responses are taking 14 days and the field is idle

  1. Quantify the impact with the RFI log: number of open items, days waiting, crews redeployed.

  2. Propose a standing coordination meeting twice a week with the engineer's decision-makers.

  3. Prioritize a short list of the 10 RFIs that block work this week.

  4. Send written notice that late responses are delaying the work.

  5. Offer practical help: a clear sketch and proposed solution with each RFI.

8.8A field story (composite)#

On a data center campus, a superintendent noticed that rework on busway connections was repeating. He asked the electrical foreman to pause, and together they torque-checked a sample of 100 connections: 11 were loose. The cause turned out to be one crew using an uncalibrated torque tool. COMMISSIONING, INTEGRATED SYSTEMS TESTING AND TURNOVER

How is the facility proven, handed over and made ready for service?

15 min read · 3,187 words

Big ideaThe big idea#

In most buildings, commissioning confirms that systems work. In a data center, commissioning is the product: the owner is selling the confidence that power and cooling will not fail. A hall is not finished when the last pipe is installed; it is finished when every system has been proven, individually and together, under realistic load and failure conditions, and the owner's team can operate it. Commissioning is also where every upstream delay lands: late utility power, late equipment, late controls. The PM must protect the testing time and prepare for it from the first month.

9.1The five levels of commissioning#

Naming varies by owner and standard, but a five-level structure is widely used in the industry. Always confirm the levels and definitions in your project's commissioning plan.

What must be ready before functional testing starts

Testing is a gate, not a calendar date. Typical prerequisites for Hall 1:

  • Permanent power energized and stable, with protection tests accepted by the utility.

  • Generators set, fueled and permitted for the planned run hours (check the air permit, Chapter 3).

  • Cooling plant filled, flushed, treated and pressure tested.

  • Fire detection and suppression installed and functionally tested to the extent required.

  • Controls and monitoring programmed, point-to-point verified, and communicating.

  • Hall cleaned and access controlled.

  • Load banks delivered, connected and tested, with enough cable and cooling capacity.

  • Safety: energization procedures, lockout rules and test permits approved.

  • All installation records and test sheets complete and accepted.

Resources for load testing (a worked example)

Load banks are large resistive (and sometimes reactive) heaters that act as the IT load before the servers arrive. To prove that Hall 1 can carry its 8 MW IT load plus the cooling load, the team may rent about 9 MW of load banks, along with cables, fuel and operators. Generators at full load consume fuel quickly; as a rough illustration, a 3 MW diesel unit may burn on the order of 200 gallons per hour at full load, so a day of tests with five units at high load can consume several thousand gallons. Plan fuel deliveries, storage capacity, spill protection, and the permit limits on run hours. Each IST day carries real cost (load banks, fuel, crews, the agent and witnesses), often in the range of $100,000 or more, which is why failed tests are expensive.

9.4Reading a functional test script#

A test script is a table of step, action, expected result and result. A short excerpt for a utility-loss test, always following the approved project procedure:

Step Action Expected result Result
1 Confirm load banks at the planned load; record starting readings Stable voltage, frequency and temperatures
2 Open the utility source to simulate loss of power UPS carries load with no interruption; alarms annunciate
3 Observe generators Generators start and accept load within the specified time (a typical requirement is about 10 seconds; verify yours)
4 Observe transfer Transfer completes; voltage and frequency remain within limits
5 Observe cooling Chillers and pumps restart per sequence; hall temperature stays within the specified range
6 Restore utility source Retransfer to utility without load loss; generators cool down and stop
7 Review alarms, logs, trends Alarm records match events; no unexpected faults

What PMs must understand: a test can fail for many reasons (equipment, installation, settings, controls logic, or the test itself). The CxA records every deviation in an issues log with severity, responsible party and required action; the team fixes and retests.

9.5The integrated systems test#

The IST is the "pull the plug" event, planned and controlled. A typical IST includes:

  • Loss of utility power at full load, with generators, UPS and cooling responding.

  • Return to normal.

  • Single-point failures: loss of one UPS module, one generator, one chiller, one pump, or one power path.

  • Maintenance scenarios: taking a system offline to prove the facility remains concurrently maintainable.

  • Fire alarm and life-safety integration: shutdown sequences, door releases, smoke control, elevator recall and notifications, as the authority requires.

  • Controls and monitoring behavior during each event.

Because the IST subjects the facility to realistic failures, it demands a detailed written procedure, approved by the owner, tenant and engineer, with safety controls, authorities, communication plans and rollback steps. The PM's role is to ensure everyone is ready, the site is safe, and the schedule allows time for retests.

9.7Payment, liquidated damages and retainage at turnover#

  • LDs stop when the ready-for-service milestone is achieved under the contract definition. Know it precisely: is it "tests passed" or "tenant accepted"? Is a punch list threshold allowed?

  • Retainage is often released hall by hall or at substantial completion, with a holdback for incomplete items, commonly 150 percent of the estimated value of listed work (check your contract).

Example: Hall 1 reaches RFS with 120 listed incomplete items valued at $0.9 million. Holdback at 150 percent is $1.35 million. If total retainage held for Hall 1's share is $4.0 million, the owner releases $2.65 million and holds $1.35 million until the list is closed.

9.8Stakeholder intervention scenarios#

SCENARIO 1A generator fails to accept load in the IST two days before the RFS date

The owner and tenant are present. The date is on every executive's mind.

  1. Stop and stabilize: make the test safe; restore normal configuration.

  2. Calm the room: state facts only; assign the CxA and the OEM to investigate immediately.

  3. Find the root cause with logs and data: control settings, fuel system, breaker timing, wiring.

  4. Estimate the fix time and the retest slot. Present the owner with the realistic new date and options to protect it (extra shifts, parallel work).

  5. Document the finding and the decision; do not hide the failure.

  6. After the fix, retest fully, not just the failed step if the correction could affect other functions.

Influence principle: in a high-stakes moment, the person who brings calm, data and a plan becomes the leader of the room.

SCENARIO 2The tenant's test team insists on criteria stricter than those in the contract

  1. Ask for the criteria in writing, and compare with the contract and the commissioning plan.

  2. If they are stricter, treat as a change: price, schedule, and risk.

  3. Offer to run the extra tests as an option with a defined scope and cost, scheduled after the contractual test is complete.

  4. Agree and record the decision with the owner.

SCENARIO 3The owner refuses to sign Hall 1 RFS because of punch items

  1. Check the contract's RFS definition and threshold.

  2. Categorize items: occupancy-blocking vs. cosmetic; identify the few that matter.

  3. Present a closure plan with dates and names; offer a holdback of 150 percent for the remainder.

  4. Ask the engineer and CxA for a written opinion on readiness.

  5. Escalate to executives if needed, with the cost of delay (LDs at $40,000 per day) on the table.

SCENARIO 4The owner's operations team says training was too short

  1. Listen and gather specifics: which systems, which gaps.

  2. Add targeted sessions, recordings, quick-reference guides, and on-call support in the first 30 days.

  3. Align with the owner's procedures so documentation is useful.

  4. Record completion and have the operations lead sign off.

10.4Influence and stakeholder management#

Chapter 1 mapped the stakeholders. Here is the working toolkit.

  1. Start with their interests, not your position. Ask: "What would a good outcome look like for you?"

  2. Bring evidence, not opinion: a photo, a drawing, a schedule excerpt, a quote, a test result.

  3. Offer choices with consequences: "If we decide by Friday we hold the date; in a month we lose three weeks."

  4. Make it easy to say yes: prepare the form, the draft, the one-page summary.

  5. Involve people early: those who help design a solution defend it.

  6. Trade fairly and keep promises. Reliability in small things is the currency of influence.

  7. Use neutral standards: a priority rule, a spec clause, an agreed schedule depersonalizes decisions.

  8. Escalate with respect, and tell people before you do.

  9. Protect relationships while protecting your rights: send the notice, then call to explain it.

Instead of Say
"Your drawings are wrong." "I found a conflict between E-402 and M-310 at row 14. Here is a sketch and two options. Which do you prefer?"
"You are late again." "The schedule needs this by Friday to protect the next trade. What is in the way, and how can I help?"
"That is not in my scope." "I read the scope as stopping at the wall. Let me show you where I see the boundary, and let us find how to cover the gap."
"The tenant keeps changing their mind." "Each change has a cost and time. Here is the list with a decision date for each. Which matter most?"

The escalation ladder: (1) direct private conversation with facts and options; (2) written follow-up copied to managers; (3) joint site meeting with decision-makers; (4) senior management meeting with a one-page brief; (5) contractual remedies, formal notice, mediation. Skip steps only for safety, legal or ethical emergencies.

Ethics of influence: be honest, transparent and in the project's interest. Never hide facts, use favors to sway officials or inspectors, or manipulate through fear. Follow your company's gift and conflict-of-interest policies and the law.

10.5Claims, disputes and delay#

Most disputes are not caused by villains; they come from unclear scope, unrecorded changes and missed notices. Prevention costs a fraction of resolution.

Type of delay Meaning Typical remedy
Excusable and compensable Caused by owner, designer or utility (as the contract allocates); you are entitled to time and money Extension of time plus delay costs
Excusable, non-compensable Outside both parties' control (some weather, force majeure) Time only
Non-excusable Contractor's own fault None; may owe LDs

10.6The casebook: twelve problems and the moves that solve them#

CASE 1A subcontractor is heading toward default

Symptoms: crews shrinking, suppliers calling about unpaid bills, late payroll rumors.

  1. Protect safety and the site; notify your project executive and counsel the same day.

  2. Read the subcontract: notice, cure period and termination rights; send the formal notice to cure exactly as required.

  3. Notify the surety if there is a bond.

  4. Stop payments except as counsel directs; verify what the sub has paid to lower tiers (unpaid suppliers can lien the project).

  5. Get replacement quotes immediately and plan the transition: work in place, materials stored, drawings.

  6. Document everything, then calculate completion cost versus remaining subcontract value for a back-charge or bond claim.

Prevent: prequalify financially; require bonds on large or risky packages; collect lien waivers monthly; do not overpay ahead of progress; watch early warning signs.

CASE 2The owner pays slowly

Check that your application was complete and on time. Find where it is stuck (architect, owner accounting, lender). Escalate politely in writing citing the payment clause and any interest. If the contract and law allow, send a notice of non-payment and, only with counsel and after the cure period, consider suspension. Protect subs through open communication. Arrange short-term financing. Prevent: learn the draw cycle at kickoff; submit early; follow up in week one.

CASE 3Equipment price spikes after award

Read the price-adjustment clause. Quantify exposure (for example, $38.0 million of generators × 6 percent = $2.28 million). Buy now if storage, financing and insurance allow; send notice within the limit; negotiate with the supplier; share the exposure plan with the owner (Chapter 4 scenario 4).

CASE 4The project is heading for a loss

Suppose the forecast shows a $5.0 million loss on a package. Do not hide it. Build a recovery plan with four levers: claims and changes you are entitled to, cost reductions (resequencing, value engineering, labor efficiency), schedule recovery that reduces general conditions, and negotiated settlements with subs or the owner. Price each with probability and owner:

Lever Amount ($M) Probability Expected ($M)
Change orders for tenant-driven density changes 2.40 70% 1.68
General conditions saved from recovering 6 working days 0.33 100% 0.33
Subcontractor back-charges 1.00 50% 0.50

CASE 5Differing site conditions

Stop work in the affected area and secure it; do not disturb the evidence until it is recorded (photos, measurements, samples, time); give written notice immediately; compare with the geotechnical report and the contract's risk clause; get the engineer's solution; continue unaffected work; track cost on a dedicated code with daily T&M tickets signed by the owner's representative.

CASE 6A critical piece of equipment fails a factory or site test

Notify the owner, engineer and manufacturer at once; do not ship or energize; investigate with the manufacturer's engineers and review the test data; agree the corrective action and retest; assess schedule options (slot swap, temporary equipment); protect warranty and contractual rights; document the root cause and check sister units.

CASE 7Labor shortage and productivity drop

Meet the sub's principal. Compare production rates with the plan. Agree a written manpower recovery plan with dates and numbers. Identify a backup subcontractor and obtain a budget quote. Look at your side: access, drawings, materials, payment. Reduce field hours through prefabrication.

CASE 8A serious safety incident

Care for the person first (emergency response, first aid, medical help). Secure the area and preserve the scene. Notify the safety manager, superintendent, project executive, owner and regulators within legal limits. Document. Investigate root cause without blame and share lessons. Resume only when hazards are controlled and the safety lead agrees. Support the worker and crew.

CASE 9Scope argument: tenant says it is included, you say it is design growth

Pull the GMP assumptions and exclusions; compare with the drawings; show the specific lines. Offer to price the item with options. Keep a neutral tone. Escalate with a one-page comparison if no agreement. Do not do the work without written direction.

CASE 10A stop-work order or notice of violation from a regulator

Stop the affected work immediately; get the notice in writing and understand what triggered it; notify the owner and executive; meet the regulator with the environmental or safety lead; correct the issue and document it; request written approval to resume; analyze why your process missed it and fix it across the site.

CASE 11Controls integration fails during commissioning

Call a joint session with all vendors, the controls lead and the commissioning agent; reproduce the failure; assign a single owner; test the interface with a written protocol; add a software freeze; reflect time and cost in the schedule and, if the cause is design or owner-driven, a notice.

CASE 12Closeout stalls and retainage is held

Get the punch list into one system with photos and due dates; separate occupancy-blocking items from cosmetic ones; hold daily closure meetings for two weeks; agree a holdback with the owner (150 percent of the value of listed work); chase documents against the closeout log; pay subs promptly when they complete their items.

10.7Your first 90 days#

Early in your career you are paid to learn fast, be reliable and make your boss's life easier. Technical brilliance matters less in year one than dependability, curiosity and the habit of closing loops.

Days 1 to 30: learn the project. Read the contract's key sections and the GMP exhibit; learn names and roles; walk the whole site with different people; master one process end to end (RFIs, submittals or the procurement log); complete safety training and ask what hazards worry the superintendent most.

Days 31 to 60: take ownership. Own a log and keep it perfectly current; run part of a meeting and send the minutes within 24 hours; find and fix one recurring friction in the information flow; volunteer for field tasks: layout checks, receiving inspections, test witnessing.

Days 61 to 90: add value. Anticipate: identify the next three problems before they arrive; help price a change or prepare part of a pay application; ask for feedback and write down what you heard; agree learning goals with your PM for the next six months.

Delta Ridge Hall 5: when the racks get hot#

Everything in Chapters 1 to 10 applies to AI data centers, but the numbers and the risks shift. A.1In plain English: what actually changes#

AI servers use powerful chips (GPUs) that draw far more electricity than traditional servers, packed into the same space. More power in means more heat out, and air alone often cannot carry that heat away. Liquid cooling brings water or a special fluid right to the chips. For the project manager, that means more power per square foot, more pipe inside the data hall, heavier racks, designs that keep changing, and a much stricter attitude to cleanliness and leaks.

Feature Conventional Delta Ridge hall AI Hall 5 (illustrative)
IT load 8 MW 12 MW
Racks and power per rack About 1,000 racks at about 8 kW About 200 racks at about 60 kW
How most heat is removed Air, through CRAH units About 75 percent by liquid at the chip, about 25 percent by air
Typical efficiency target (PUE) About 1.3 About 1.2
Piping in the white space Little Extensive: manifolds, hoses, quick connections

A.2The liquid cooling chain, drawn#

  • Heat plant: chillers, dry coolers or cooling towers reject heat to the outside.

  • Facility loop: large pipes and pumps carry water between the heat plant and the hall.

  • CDU (coolant distribution unit): a heat exchanger and pump skid that separates the building water from the clean fluid that touches the servers, and controls its temperature and flow.

  • Rack manifold: splits the fluid into many small flows to each server.

  • Cold plates: metal plates on the chips that move heat into the fluid. Other approaches include rear-door heat exchangers and immersion in a bath of fluid; the project team must know which one the tenant has chosen.

A.3A worked example: sizing Hall 5#

Heat and cooling. The hall has 12 MW of IT load plus about 0.6 MW of electrical losses.

Cooling tons = 12,600 kW ÷ 3.517 ≈ 3,580

  • Heat captured by liquid at the chips: 75 percent of 12.0 MW = 9.0 MW (about 2,560 tons).

  • Heat left to air: 3.0 MW from the servers plus 0.6 MW of losses = 3.6 MW (about 1,020 tons).

  • Heat plant: with 650-ton units, six carry the load (6 × 650 = 3,900 tons), and a seventh gives N+1: seven units.

Power. At a PUE of 1.2, the peak facility load is 12 × 1.2 = 14.4 MW. With 3.0 MW generators, five carry the load (15.0 MW) and a sixth gives N+1: six generators, compared with five for an 8 MW conventional hall.

A.5The new craft: cleanliness and leak control#

Liquid in the white space is the biggest cultural change for a data center crew.

Cleanliness. The fluid loops that touch servers are sensitive to dirt, welding debris and contamination. Typical practice includes:

  1. Fabricate and store pipe clean and capped.

  2. Protect open ends at all times; no cutting or grinding near open systems.

  3. Flush the loops with a defined procedure until particle counts meet the specification.

  4. Filter the fluid and check its chemistry before connection to servers.

  5. Record every flush, test and sample with signed forms.

Leak control.

  • Pressure test every section before insulation closes or manifolds connect to servers.

  • Leak detection: sensors, drip trays and drainage in the right places, wired to the monitoring system.

  • Isolation: valves that can shut a section quickly; a written response plan for who does what when an alarm sounds.

  • Handling rules for quick connections: training, tool control, and a clean-room discipline.

  • Test the response: include a simulated leak alarm in commissioning.

A.6The power side: density and swings#

  • Higher currents per rack mean larger busway, bigger breakers, more heat in electrical rooms, and stricter arc-flash controls. Verify clearances and labeling against the actual equipment.

  • Fast load changes: AI workloads can ramp and drop quickly. The electrical engineer must study how UPS systems, generators and the utility respond, and the commissioning plan should include tests that reflect the tenant's real load pattern. Ask the tenant for a load profile early.

  • Utility coordination matters more: the utility must accept the size and the ramp of the load. Agree the energization steps in writing.

A.7Stakeholder intervention scenarios#

SCENARIO 1The tenant changes the cooling approach after the design freeze

The tenant wants to move from rear-door heat exchangers to direct-to-chip cooling in Hall 5, because their new servers require it.

  1. Do not say no. Say you will return in three working days with the impact.

  2. Map the ripple: CDUs, piping, manifolds, controls, electrical for pumps, floor loads, commissioning scripts, procurement already placed.

  3. Price decision dates and make them visible:

Decision timing Cost effect (illustrative) Schedule effect
Before design freeze Redesign only None
After design freeze but before CDU orders About $3.5M About 1 week
After CDU orders About $6.5M including cancellation and re-order About 10 weeks

  1. Ask the tenant to choose and to sign a change request with the date.

  2. Protect the rest of the project: do not let the change erode the schedule for Halls 1 to 4 without agreement.

Influence principle: a decision with a visible price and a visible deadline gets made.

SCENARIO 2Debris is found in the chilled-fluid loop during flushing

  1. Stop connecting to servers. Isolate the section.

  2. Find the source: welding, cutting, open pipe ends, installation sequence.

  3. Re-flush and re-test; document particle counts.

  4. Fix the process: pipe caps, hot work controls, supervision, pre-task talks.

  5. Tell the tenant the facts and the new date; offer them a witness.

SCENARIO 3The tenant's servers are arriving six weeks earlier than planned

  1. Verify the date and the quantity; find which racks and loops they need first.

  2. Rebuild the schedule for a smaller first block that is truly ready.

  3. Price acceleration options (second shift for the first block, early commissioning of one CDU group).

  4. Define a safe early access plan as in Chapter 5, scenario 1.

  5. Record the decision and who pays.

Myth Reality
"Liquid cooling is just plumbing." It is a clean, controlled, tested system where small defects cause big failures

A.12Your first moves on an AI project#

  1. Ask the tenant for the cooling approach, rack power, rack weight and load profile in writing.

  2. Draw the liquid chain for your project and name the supplier of each element.

  3. Write the cleanliness and flushing procedure and have the engineer and tenant approve it.
